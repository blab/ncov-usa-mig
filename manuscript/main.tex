\begin{abstract}
The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic resulted in an enormous quantity of genomic surveillance data, which can enable us to reconstruct detailed transmission patterns.
However, the complexity of analyzing these data across large geographic and temporal windows has remained a challenge.
Rather than reconstructing a phylogenetic tree to estimate migration, we can use shared genetic proximity of sequences as a marker of transmission across demes.
Here, we use 5,765,925 SARS-CoV-2 genomes from Canada, Mexico, and the United States (US) to identify pairs of identical sequences which describe transmission patterns across geography and age.
We describe networks of transmission down to US states and Canadian provinces and compare how different measures of mobility and travel predict transmission between US states and regions.
We extend our approach to measure transmission changes over time and demonstrate that international transmission remained suppressed in 2021 even after domestic cross-regional transmission had increased. 
Finally, we find age-related transmission clusters and capture the transition at age 18 from a school-aged contact network to a young adult network.
In summary, this study highlights how we can capture fine-scale transmission patterns using large genome datasets and improve our understanding of pathogen spread across geographies and time.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Genomic surveillance has become a fundamental tool for understanding pathogen spread and evolution.
In particular, the COVID-19 pandemic has led to the sequencing of SARS-CoV-2 at an unprecedented rate, with 16.7 million genomes submitted to GISAID's EpiCoV database from December 2019 through December 2024.\autocite{khareGISAIDsRolePandemic2021}
Data at this scale should theoretically make it possible to study transmission in incredible detail.
Phylodynamics methods leveraging such sequence data typically rely on ancestral tree reconstruction to recover transmission history and population-level mixing patterns.\autocite{dengGenomicSurveillanceReveals2020,neherNextfluRealtimeTracking2015}
These tree-based approaches are powerful, but they scale poorly because the number of possible phylogenies increases factorially with the number of genomes, limiting analyses to thousands or tens of thousands of sequences.
The number of sequences that can be included in an analysis limits the resolution at which transmission can be studied.
Characterizing spread patterns between $n$ population groups indeed involves inferring transmission between $\sim n^2$ pairs of groups, and sequence data requirements become substantial as the number of groups increase.
Overcoming these limitations would thus make it possible to study spread across many fine-grained groups and better understand complex transmission structure.

Statistical methods that identify structure across genomes without building phylogenies \autocite{mattesonGenomicSurveillanceReveals2023,sobkowiakCov2clustersGenomicClustering2022,cahuantziUnsupervisedIdentificationSignificant2024,nanduriDimensionalityReductionDistills2024} offer a promising avenue.
In prior work, we demonstrated that the composition of clusters of identical sequences is informative about transmission between population groups.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}
Because pathogens accumulate mutations along transmission chains, identical sequences tend to capture epidemiologically linked individuals, and the rate at which two groups share identical sequences reflects the intensity of transmission between them.
We introduced an identical sequence relative risk metric ($RR_{seq}$) that measures how the rate at which identical sequences are observed between two groups compares with what is expected under random mixing while accounting for sequencing effort.
Accounting for sequencing effort also lets us leverage the full dataset, including less-sampled groups.
We used this framework to map county-level geographic patterns of SARS-CoV-2 spread in Washington State from over 100,000 sequences.
Given this approach only relies on genetic distance calculations, it has the potential to scale to even larger datasets and reconstruct transmission patterns at high resolution.

Here, we analyze patterns of occurrence of identical sequences from 5.7 million publicly available SARS-CoV-2 genomes from North America (Canada, Mexico, and the United States (US)) collected between January 2020 and December 2024.
We investigate transmission between 62 North American subdivisions: US states, Canadian provinces, and Mexico.
This granularity enables us to characterize transmission structure comprehensively across a continent through time, and to understand how it relates to administrative boundaries typically used for aggregation.
We also investigate how different types of mobility indicators recapitulate transmission at different spatial scales.
Finally, we characterize transmission between age groups at single-year resolution, a level of detail not previously achieved from either epidemiological or genomic data, and difficult to reach with methods relying on more divergent genomes.\autocite{tran-kiemCharacterizingInformativenessPathogen2026}
The scale of this dataset reveals structure that subsampling or aggregation would obscure.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Description of EpiCoV Data}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{desc.pdf}
    \caption{
        Characteristics of publicly available SARS-CoV-2 sequences in North America from January 2020 to December 2024.
        A: Number of sequences available in the GISAID database for each North American subdivision.
        B: Weekly sequences per 100,000 persons by country with a rolling 3-week centered simple moving average.
        C: Number of sequences with valid age metadata by age.
        D: Age distribution of sequences by geographic location among sequences with valid age and geographic location information.
    }
    \label{fig:desc}
\end{figure}

Between January 2020 and December 2024, 6,187,049 SARS-CoV-2 genomes were deposited in the GISAID EpiCoV database across Canada, the US and Mexico (hereafter referred to as North America).\autocite{khareGISAIDsRolePandemic2021}
We define North American subdivisions as states in the US (including the District of Columbia but excluding all other territories), provinces in Canada and the country of Mexico based on the available geographic resolution in the sequence metadata for these 3 countries.
Among these genomes, 5,765,925 have valid subdivision metadata and sequencing coverage greater than 90\%.
Figure \ref{fig:desc}A displays the geographic distribution of sequences by North American subdivisions.
We find considerable heterogeneity in sequence volume by subdivision.
California has the highest number of sequences available with 935,874 sequences and Prince Edward Island (the smallest Canadian province), has the lowest with 1,148 sequences.
When adjusting for population size, sequencing effort still varies across subdivision with a median of 1469 sequences per 100,000 persons (IQR: 935 - 2289).
Wyoming has the highest per capita sequence count of any subdivision (5,936 sequences per 100,000 persons) while Mexico has the lowest number of sequences per capita of any subdivision (76.5 sequences per 100,000 persons).
Looking across countries, all of them had similar sequence count trajectories, with the number of sequences per capita increasing in early 2021 and peaking in late 2021/early 2022 following the Delta and Omicron waves (Fig.\ \ref{fig:desc}B) and sequencing intensity decreasing in all three countries by 2024.
Mexico consistently had the lowest number of sequences per capita of the three countries (Fig.\ \ref{fig:desc}B).
Among the 5.7 million sequences available in North America, 52.0\% have valid age metadata.
The full age distribution is shown in Figure \ref{fig:desc}C.
Age groups are well represented across regions (Figure \ref{fig:desc}D), with Canadian regions tending to skew older compared to the US regions.


\subsection{Relationship between Geography and Identical Sequences}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{geo_clust.pdf}

    Each row's subunit is designated using the standard 2 letter US state/Canadian provincial codes with Mexico represented as MX.

    In both panels $RR_{seq}$ is colored according to a logarithmic scale.
    }
    \label{fig:geo_clust}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{geo_pc.pdf}
    \caption{Continental structure of SARS-CoV-2 transmission in North America from dimensionality reduction.
    A: Geographic regions used to annotate results of the clustering analysis.
    Sequences from the Yukon, Northwest, and Nunavut Territories in Canada were not available, so these territories were not assigned to any region.
    B: Principal coordinate analysis using a dissimilarity metric between subdivisions based on $RR_{seq}$ values.

    Percentages indicate the proportion of variance explained by the first two principal coordinates (PC1 and PC2).
    }
    \label{fig:geo_pc}
\end{figure}

We calculate $RR_{seq}$ scores between all North American subdivisions to identify spatial patterns of transmission.
Figure \ref{fig:geo_clust}A shows choropleth maps that illustrate patterns for a few focal subdivisions (Ontario province, New York state, and Mexico).
In all three examples, $RR_{seq}$ values are highest within the focal subdivision and $RR_{seq}$ tends to decay with distance, highlighting that transmission increases with geographic proximity.
Despite New York state and Ontario being adjacent, we do not find evidence for enriched transmission between these two subdivisions ($RR_{seq}^{NY,ON} = 0.40 < 1$, with an uncertainty interval of 0.37-0.42).
By contrast, we find $RR_{seq}$ scores greater than 1 (indicating enrichment) between Mexico and several US states including California, Texas, New Mexico, and Oklahoma.
To evaluate whether this difference in cross border pattern could be an artifact from not being able to split Mexico into subdivisions, we perform a sensitivity analysis aggregating Canada into a single unit and find $RR_{seq} < 1$ across all states (max $RR_{seq}$ of 0.26).
This suggests that transmission is not fully described by proximity alone and that administrative (here national) boundaries may further shape transmission risk.

To further characterize geographic structure in SARS-CoV-2 transmission in North America, we perform hierarchical clustering using a distance measure based on $RR_{seq}$ values between subdivisions.
Figure \ref{fig:geo_clust}B represents a heatmap of pairwise $RR_{seq}$ between subdivisions with rows and columns ordered based on hierarchical clustering results.
Rows are further annotated based on geographic regions defined in Figure \ref{fig:geo_pc}A.
We identify a block structure along the diagonal, where blocks correspond to groups of subdivisions with elevated rate of identical sequence occurrence.
These blocks can be interpreted as relevant transmission units for SARS-CoV-2 and generally match existing geographic regions.
For example, Eastern Canada, Western Canada, Mideast, and Plains regions have all of their subdivisions sit on contiguous branches of the hierarchical cluster tree.
By contrast, a few US regions do not perfectly sort along regional definitions.
For example, states from the Southeast do not form a single transmission unit and are split into different smaller clusters (shown in depth in Supplemental Figure \ref{fig:hclust_subset}):
(1) the District of Columbia (DC), Maryland, Virginia, and North Carolina cluster with the Mideast and New England states;
(2) Arkansas, Louisiana, and Mississippi group with Southwestern states and Mexico;
and (3) the remaining states cluster with states from the Great Lakes area.
We observe a similar pattern in the Far West, with Alaska, Oregon and Washington displaying greater transmission link with Idaho and Utah (Rocky Mountain), while California, Hawaii, and Nevada rather cluster with Arizona and Texas.

To investigate whether transmission patterns reflect continental geographic structure, we conduct dimensionality reduction using principal coordinate analysis (Fig.\ \ref{fig:geo_pc}B), using the same dissimilarity matrix as the hierarchical clustering.
The first principal coordinate (PC1) cleanly partitions Canadian provinces from US states, with Mexico close to the US cloud but well separated from the Canadian provinces.
The second principal coordinate (PC2) corresponds broadly to an East-West axis.
Although these first two coordinates map well onto geographic structure, they only capture $\sim$30\% of the total variance in identical sequence occurrence patterns.
This indicates that, while geographic proximity between subdivisions is an important determinant of transmission risk, it is insufficient to explain the full structure of transmission.


\subsection{Temporal and Seasonal Relationships for Geographic RR}

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{geo_time.pdf}
    \caption{Temporal changes in spatial patterns of SARS-CoV-2 identical sequence transmission in North America.
    A: Distribution of $nRR_{seq}$ for each calendar quarter stratified by within region (red), domestic between region (green), and international pairings (navy).
    B: Heatmap of fold change in $nRR_{seq}$ relative to the first quarter of 2023 (2023 Q1) by quarter (y-axis) with each column representing a subdivision pairing.
    C: Cross-region pairs of US states with persistent high $nRR_{seq}$ scores.
    Left: Persistent cross-region connections, defined as $nRR_{seq}$  in the top 2\%ile for at least 5 quarters; line width shows the number of quarters with a high connection.
    Right: Persistent seasonal connections, stratified by quarter to examine seasonal trends. Each map shows state pairs with $nRR_{seq}$ within the top 2\%ile for at least 2 years; line width shows the number of such quarters.}
    \label{fig:geo_time}
\end{figure}

In order to investigate temporal spread patterns across North American subdivisions, we calculate quarterly $RR_{seq}$ scores normalized by the average of within subdivision $RR_{seq}$ scores.
This normalization step enables comparison across time periods and account for changes in transmission intensity across time periods (see Methods).
Normalized $RR_{seq}$ ($nRR_{seq}$) were highly variable in Q1 2020 and rapidly decreased to a minimum in 2020 Q4 (Fig.\ \ref{fig:geo_time}A), indicating a global reduction of across subdivision transmission.
Across subdivisions transmission slowly increased during 2021 and reached a peak during 2022.
Throughout all time periods, subdivisions within the same region have higher normalized relative risk scores compared to subdivisions in different regions and countries.
For each pair of subdivisions, we also calculate fold-change of $nRR_{seq}$ with respect to 2023 Q1 to further track temporal changes in transmission patterns (Fig.\ \ref{fig:geo_time}B).
We again see a sharp decrease in transmission across most subdivision pairs from 2020 Q1 to 2020 Q2 which persists through the remainder of 2020.
The $nRR_{seq}$ scores began to increase in a gradated fashion during 2021 as within region nRR scores increased in early 2021 and between region nRR scores increased in late 2021.
International normalized $RR_{seq}$ scores remained low throughout 2021 and had a delayed rise in 2022.
These results highlight distinct temporal dynamics across spatial scales, with local transmission increasing before longer distance spread likely due to changes in public health interventions and behaviors.

Given that we mainly identify within region pairs as enriched for identical sequences, we investigate whether relatively high scoring $nRR_{seq}$ scores can be used to identify between region subdivision pairs with significant transmission throughout the study period.
We define persistent cross-region connections as pairs of non-neighboring subdivisions in different regions whose $nRR_{seq}$ scores were above the 98th percentile (i.e.\ top second percentile) for at least 5 of the 20 quarters encompassing our study time period (shown in Fig.\ \ref{fig:geo_time}C).
We find 21 between subdivision pairings meeting these criteria, and all of these are between US states.
Florida has the most persistent cross-region connections (9) followed by New York and New Jersey (both with 6 connections).
This suggests that Florida acted as a major hub for inter-regional transmission, especially between the Southeast and the Mideast regions.
We then stratify this analysis by quarters to look for cross-region subdivision pairs which had at least 2 years of high scoring $nRR_{seq}$ for each quarter.
We find that the number of persistent seasonal connections varies by season with Q1 (January - March) and Q3 (July - September) having the most persistent seasonal connections and Q2 (April - June) having the least.
Florida again has the most persistent seasonal connections across all 4 quarters, with the majority of these connections being with Mideast states (New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and New York), but also with Texas and California across multiple quarters.
Florida's persistent seasonal connections in every quarter highlight the importance it plays in between region transmission patterns across the United States.

\subsection{Geographical Predictors of Transmission}

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{geo_dist.pdf}
    \caption{Relationship between identical sequence RR in the US and movement metrics across scales.
    A: Relationship between identical sequence RR and distance between states and RR of movements between states.
    Solid line indicates LOESS fit and we report Pearson correlation coefficient $r$ at the top of each plot.
    B: Deviance explained in the $RR_{seq}$ obtained from generalized additive model (GAM) including different combinations of travel/movement and geographic distance predictors.
    Blue bars correspond to model including an interaction term between distance and the travel/movement predictor.
    C: Predicted identical sequence RR from GAM models with interaction terms between distance and movement predictors.
    Colored curves represent the predicted $RR_{seq}$ when fixing the CBSA distance between states.
    We only depict predicted $RR_{seq}$ for values of the RR of movement observed around the distance band of interest.
    To determine the range of expected RR of movement at a given distance, we used the 2nd and 98th percentiles of the movement RR observed for pairs of US states with a distance close to the distance of interest (100 km: 0-250 km, 500 km: 250-1000 km, 2000 km: 1000-3000 km, 4000 km: 3000-5000 km).
    }
    \label{fig:geo_dist}
\end{figure}

To understand the drivers of these geographic relationships, we investigate which movement and distance variables predict $RR_{seq}$.
The variables we investigate include the minimum core-based statistical area (CBSA) distance between states, the number of ground travel trips between states measured in the National Household Travel Survey (NHTS), the timeshare proportion of smartphone users traveling across state lines, and the number of air travel passengers traveling between states using the US Department of Transportation's Airline Origin and Destination Survey (DB1B).\autocite{NationalHouseholdTravel,DB1B_Dataset,pullanoCharacterizingUSSpatial2025}
We limit this analysis to the continental United States as most of the data sources used are US specific, with Hawaii and Alaska are outliers for all distance and movement metrics due to their geographic isolation.
To facilitate comparisons, we calculate log-transformed relative risk scores for smartphone mobility, air travel trips and ground travel trips, while keeping geographic distance on a linear scale.
For air travel, we exclude state pairs with no recorded airline trips in the DB1B dataset from the analysis.
We show the results of our analysis in Figure \ref{fig:geo_dist}A.
Distance between states is negatively correlated with $RR_{seq}$ with a biphasic pattern; there is a rapid descent in $RR_{seq}$ from 0 to 500 km and then a slower descent phase beyond 500 km.
This distance coincides with state pairs transitioning from primarily being within the same region to across different regions.
Ground travel and smartphone mobility RR are both positively correlated with $RR_{seq}$.
Across the variables we considered, air travel displays the weakest relationship with $RR_{seq}$ (Pearson $r$ = 0.12).

Given the correlation between movement/travel and geographic distance (Supplemental Fig.\ \ref{fig:pred_corr}), we tested for potential interactions.
First we calculate the Pearson correlation coefficient of each travel variable with $RR_{seq}$ before and after controlling for CBSA distance (Supplemental Table \ref{tab:partial_corr}).
For these analyses, we remove pairs with significant outliers for the travel/movement measures (i.e. values greater than 3 standard deviations away from the mean value) as these mostly correspond to pairs with sparse mobility data, resulting in noisy estimates.
The correlation of $RR_{seq}$ with ground travel RR decreases from 0.682 to 0.252 after controlling for distance while the correlation for smartphone mobility decreases from 0.545 to 0.024, indicating the relationship of these movement variables with $RR_{seq}$ heavily overlaps with the relationship explained by distance.
In contrast, air travel has a more modest decrease in correlation from 0.284 to 0.222.
We then calculate correlations stratified on CBSA distance shown in Table \ref{tab:band_corr}.
Ground travel and smartphone mobility RR scores are most strongly correlated with $RR_{seq}$ for short distance pairs (0-250 km) with both $r > 0.6$.
For long distance pairs (3000-5000 km) ground travel still remains significantly correlated, but smartphone mobility data becomes uncorrelated at moderate distances (1000-3000 km) and anti-correlated at long distances (3000-5000 km).
Therefore, our ability to predict transmission relationships based on smartphone mobility data appears limited to short distance state pairings, while ground travel has a stronger signal across a wider geographic range.
Air travel is anti-correlated to $RR_{seq}$ at short distances and most strongly related to it at long distances.
Notably it has the strongest correlation of the three variables for all distance categories greater than 250 km, supporting that air travel data can be helpful in predicting long distance transmission patterns.
Based on these findings, we develop generalized additive models (GAMs) with tensor products to look at the interaction of ground travel and air travel with CBSA distance.
The relative performance comparing GAM models with and without distance is shown in Figure \ref{fig:geo_dist}B and observed vs fitted plots for all the GAM models are shown in Supplemental Figure \ref{fig:obs_exp}.
The distance only model performs the best of all the single predictor models with 50\% of deviance explained followed by ground travel at 40\%.
We find that adding an interaction term decreases RMSE and increases the percentage of deviance explained for ground travel, air travel and smartphone mobility models, with log likelihood ratio testing confirming that these interaction terms are significant (Supplemental Table \ref{tab:interaction_lrt}).
Predictions of $RR_{seq}$ from the interaction models are shown in Figure \ref{fig:geo_dist}C.
Importantly, these models are only plotted over the range of the travel RRs that existed for each distance band, and can not be extrapolated beyond the plotted extent.
For both variables, shorter distance pairs at 100 km have the highest predicted $RR_{seq}$ while longer distance pairs at 2000 km and 4000 km curves predict lower $RR_{seq}$, regardless of the travel RR.
For ground travel, we find a generally positive and monotonic relationship with predicted $RR_{seq}$ across most distances.
The one exception is the 500 km curve, which remains generally flat and centered around $RR_{seq} = 1$.
Air travel shows relatively flat curves for 100 km and 2000 km, a modest positively correlated curve for 500 km, and the strongest positive curve for 4000 km.
This again suggests that air travel is most predictive for transmission at longer distances.
Overall, this work highlights the complex interplay between distance and travel and their relationship with transmission.

\subsection{Age Relationships with Identical Sequence Transmission}

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{age.pdf}
    \caption{Patterns of SARS-CoV-2 transmission between age groups in North America.
    A: Matrix of pairwise $RR_{seq}$ between age groups in 1-year bins, ages 0--80.
    B: Matrix of $RR_{seq}$ zoomed on ages 0--24 using an expanded color scale (RR 0.5--2); the 18-year-old row and column are outlined in black and the asterisk marks the 18y/18y cell.
    C: Matrix of $RR_{seq}$ between age groups for pairs within the same subdivision, across subdivisions within a region, and across regions; color scale as in A.
    D: $RR_{seq}$ deviance stratified by geography, colored as in C, with GAM best-fit lines.
    Higher deviance indicates greater departure from random mixing, with zero corresponding to the expectation under random mixing.
    }
    \label{fig:age}
\end{figure}

Using a very large number of sequences enables us to describe high resolution transmission patterns between age groups, down to single-year identical sequence relative risks.
We create a full $RR_{seq}$ heatmap across all ages from 0 to 80, shown in Figure \ref{fig:age}A, and find several notable age patterns.
Across all ages we find the highest $RR_{seq}$ scores to be among same age pairings (i.e. the heatmap's diagonal) and adjacent age groups, highlighting that transmission rates are highest between individuals of similar ages.
Due to the risk of samples from the same individual partly inflating enrichment along the diagonal, we complete a sensitivity analysis combining age and gender to confirm that same-age enrichment persists among different-gender pairs and how inflated same-age same-gender $RR_{seq}$ scores are by comparison (see Supplemental Fig.\ \ref{fig:age_gender}).
We control for this by flagging and removing identical sequence pairs that share metadata within a 3-day window from the RR calculation (see Discussion).
For children (ages < 18 years) we find a very strong transmission cluster with $RR_{seq} > 1$ for nearly all age pairs, except for infants (i.e. 0-year-olds), who have a much smaller transmission cluster limited to ages 0-2.
The younger age findings are shown in-depth in Figure \ref{fig:age}B.
Within this range, $RR_{seq}$ is highest for same-age pairings and then tends to decay across 3-5 years from the diagonal, with a clear boundary at ages $> 17$.
Additionally, there is a weaker cluster of sequence enrichment between children and adults from 30 to 50 years of age, creating parallel ``secondary diagonals'' resulting from transmission between parental and child age groups.
For 18-year-olds there is a clear transition in transmission patterns away from children and towards young adults.
We find a discrete block of enrichment for 18- to 22-year-olds, with $RR_{seq}$ greater than 1.1 for all pairs in this discrete block.
This block corresponds to the typical age of university students across North America and when adults generally enter the workforce if they do not pursue tertiary education.
From age 23 onwards, the enrichment pattern becomes similar to the general adult population with a narrow band of 1-3 years around the diagonal where $RR_{seq}$ remains greater than 1.1 and then a wider band of $RR_{seq} \approx 1$.
Finally, for age 60 years and onwards we see another transmission cluster emerge, with $RR_{seq}$ increasing with age.
The identified transmission clusters seem to correspond to age contact behaviors, and we compare our $RR_{seq}$ scores to existing synthetic contact data (Supplemental Fig.\ \ref{fig:age_contact}).\autocite{mistryInferringHighresolutionHuman2021}
Both for Canada ($r=0.60$) and the USA ($r=0.72$), $RR_{seq}$ is log-log correlated to the contact data, supporting this hypothesis.

Additionally, we investigate how $RR_{seq}$ patterns across age groups vary by geographic levels.
We stratify the age $RR_{seq}$ heatmaps in Figure \ref{fig:age}C by same subdivision pairs, different subdivision but same region pairs and different region pairs.
For the same subdivision age heatmap, the structure largely matches our main age heatmap with a prominent diagonal of $RR_{seq} > 1.5$, and distinct clusters for children, university aged young adults and elderly adults.
As we look at more distant geographic connections, we find that the age structure becomes less prominent with the central diagonal reverting to $RR_{seq} = 1$ for adults and greater mixing of the pediatric and elderly clusters with the general population.
In order to systematically summarize how much a given age group's $RR_{seq}$ profile deviates from random mixing, we create a deviance score.
This is calculated as the root-mean-square of the age group's log-transformed RR scores:

\[D_A = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{B} \left( \log_{10} RR_{seq}^{A,B} \right)^2}\]

Where $A$ and $B$ represent age groups in the population.
By squaring the log-transformed $RR_{seq}$, enrichment ($RR_{seq} > 1$) and depletion ($RR_{seq} < 1$) contribute equally to the deviance.
We expect that if for age $A$'s identical sequences are fully randomly mixed (i.e. $RR_{seq}^{A,B} = 1$ across all paired ages $B$) that the deviance, $D_A$, would equal 0.
As transmission becomes more age structured, the deviance will increase.
We show the deviance for each age category across different spatial scales in Figure \ref{fig:age}D.
The same subdivision deviance curve shows age structured transmission is highest for the young and the elderly, with the minimum in middle adulthood.
For broader geographic scales, we see deviance remains highest among the young and elderly age groups, but the magnitude of deviance scores are decreased compared to the same subdivision curve across all ages.
These deviance curves are evidence that age structure plays a stronger role in transmission patterns at local geographies, whereas age structure appears less important to describe transmission at larger geographic scales.

\section{Discussion}

Leveraging 5.7 million SARS-CoV-2 genomes, we characterized transmission across North America at the resolution of individual states, provinces, and single-year age groups.
This high resolution analysis provides a deeper and richer understanding than would be available with traditional phylogeographic methods.
We described how transmission networks span across administrative units in North America and highlighted the subtleties of the relationships between movement, distance, and infections.
Furthermore, we identified how transmission dynamics varied as movement networks changed over the course of the pandemic.
Finally, fine-scale age analyses highlighted the social units of transmission such as schools, universities, and households, and we reconstructed the age contact network based on shared identical sequences.

Conducting a large scale analysis of pathogen transmission between North American subdivisions leveraging millions of sequences is not feasible with traditional phylogeographic methods.
In prior work, we showed that patterns of occurrence of identical sequences between groups can be used to characterize transmission patterns using a large SARS-CoV-2 sequences dataset of 114,298 sequences from Washington state.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}
To scale this up to a multinational analysis, we faced a major computational challenge.
While a relative risk calculation typically is computationally easy, finding the Hamming distance between all possible pairs of sequences can be quite costly and grows quadratically with the number of sequences.
Recalculating this pair list with each analysis would be highly cumbersome and would limit analysis to only be conducted on a high-performance computing (HPC) machine or cluster.
We overcame these challenges with a one-time preprocessing step to identify the identical sequence pairs and then store this information in a relational database.
This list links to a metadata table allowing the structure to be reused for a variety of different RR analyses and subsetted as needed (e.g. sliding windows of identical sequences for temporal analyses).
Relational databases are ideal as they allow for fast queries of this identical sequence data structure while not loading the entire dataset into memory for each calculation.
While the preprocessing step does require significant computing power, the actual relative risk calculations and downstream analyses can be done on typical modern machines.
Details of this technique and the hardware that we used to run this analysis can be found in the Supplementary Information.

One major benefit of being able to conduct these analyses so efficiently is that we are able to do high resolution geographic analyses even across a large area.
Traditional phylogeographic approaches would not be able to process a sufficient number of sequences to describe the relationships between all 62 geographic units in our analyses.
Instead, approaches have typically needed to either focus on a smaller study area for a high resolution analysis or subsample data from a larger area and then aggregate locations.\autocite{mattesonGenomicSurveillanceReveals2023,dengGenomicSurveillanceReveals2020,lemeyUntanglingIntroductionsPersistence2021,tsuiGenomicAssessmentInvasion2023}
A drawback of this approach is that aggregation assumes homogeneity within populations and can mask important relationships.
For example, one might assume that states within the same pre-defined US region share similar transmission patterns given their geographic or economic proximity.
However, when we conducted the RR analysis at the regional level instead of the subdivision level (Supplemental Fig.\ \ref{fig:reg_hmap}) the Far West had no $RR_{seq}$ scores > 1 with any other regions, despite individual states having $RR_{seq} > 1$ with geographies outside the same region (e.g. Washington and Idaho, California and Mexico). (Fig.\ \ref{fig:geo_clust})
This highlights how geographic aggregation can miss the actual scale and structure where transmission occurs.

An important example of the significance of scale can be seen with the ``Acela Corridor'' in the United States, where five major metropolitan areas span the northeastern coast: Boston, MA; New York, NY; Philadelphia, PA; Baltimore, MD and Washington DC.
Since US metropolitan areas often cross state boundaries, this can lead them to spill across regions as well.
Based on this we expected Connecticut (within the New England region) to have high levels of transmission with New York (Mideast) due to having a large commuting population to New York City.
Similarly, we expected Maryland (within the Mideast) to have high levels of transmission with Virginia (Southeast) as they both are part of the Washington DC metropolitan area.
Analyzing patterns of occurrence of identical sequences between these states confirmed our expectations (Fig.\ \ref{fig:geo_clust}).
When we zoomed our hierarchical clustering tree on the New England, Mideast, and Southeast region (Supplemental Fig.\ \ref{fig:hclust_subset}), we found that the states from Connecticut through South Carolina sit on closely linked branches of the hierarchical tree.
Despite Connecticut being a part of New England, its RR pattern more closely aligns with states that are part of the New York, NY metro areas.
Similarly, we found more transmission between Virginia, North Carolina and South Carolina and the Washington DC metro than with other Southeastern states.
Identification of these metropolitan transmission clusters matches with prior work that discusses the importance of city and metropolitan areas as spatial units for analysis.\autocite{starkLocalVariationsSpatial2012,dalzielUrbanizationHumidityShape2018}
Ideally we would have directly explored metropolitan or county level transmission patterns, but sub-state/sub-provincial location data was sparse and inconsistently annotated.
Improved availability of more granular geographic resolution such as US counties or Canadian census divisions would allow us to further delineate relevant cross-state transmission patterns.

Another key geographic finding of our work was that the relationship between movement/travel variables and SARS-CoV-2 was highly dependent on distance.
Smartphone mobility data, air travel flows, and commuting flows have been used to model the spread of SARS-CoV-2 and the timing of importation events.\autocite{kuoAirlineTransportationArrival2021,jiaPopulationFlowDrives2020,zhanModelingPrediction20192020,badrAssociationMobilityPatterns2020a,changMobilityNetworkModels2021}
These models do well at empirically describing the epidemic curves for SARS-CoV-2, but there remains a gap to describe the exact relationship between distance/mobility with true virus transmission.
Leveraging genomic data and using the $RR_{seq}$ between US states, we showed what are the different distance regimes where each variable is pertinent.
We found that smartphone mobility data performed well at short distances, but rapidly lost predictive power for longer distance state pairs.
In contrast, the ground trip data from the NHTS was robust across both short and long distance pairs.
We hypothesize that states may be too coarse of a geographical unit for the smartphone mobility data to perform optimally, and ideally we would repeat this analysis at a finer scale such as county to see if it outperforms the ground trip dataset.
Air travel was associated with transmission for long distance pairs only, with its predictive power surpassing ground travel's in our stratified long range analysis.
This is notable as there has been long-standing debate regarding the importance of air travel networks versus commuting networks in modeling domestic respiratory virus spread within the United States.\autocite{brownsteinEmpiricalEvidenceEffect2006, viboudSynchronyWavesSpatial2006,charuHumanMobilitySpatial2017}
Our findings agree with a recent phylogeographic analysis of influenza seasons, which found that ground trips were the strongest predictor of state-to-state transmission, while air travel still contributed additional viral migration.\autocite{dejongCompetitionTransmissionLineages2025} 
Notably, our models suggest that the single strongest predictor of $RR_{seq}$ is geographic distance, matching recent work that showed distance between CBSAs was one of the major drivers of spread during outbreak waves.\autocite{callierSpatiotemporalSpreadCOVID192026}
Nevertheless, we found that air and ground travel had persistent significant partial correlation with $RR_{seq}$ after adjusting for distance.(Supplemental Table \ref{tab:partial_corr})
Future epidemiological models likely would benefit from combining multiple of these movement/travel variables and weighting them by distance to better estimate transmission between cities versus between states/provinces.

The large number of sequences we used in this analysis allowed us to describe age transmission down to single-year age groups, which allows a particularly detailed investigation of how age related social structures affect transmission.
The most significant examples of this in our study were of children and young adults.
The $RR_{seq}$ heatmap demonstrated a clear transition point in the transmission matrix with 18-year-olds clustering more tightly to university-age young adults instead of with secondary school teenagers.
Similarly, there was a more gradual transition in transmission patterns from age 22 to 23 which corresponds to when many young adults enter the general workforce.
The distinct cluster from 18 to 22 years likely is due in part to the unique transmission environment of university life with its relative social isolation from other age groups, although we cannot directly determine the housing status and education/work background from the metadata provided.
Additionally, we were able to capture signal of a parent-child ``secondary diagonal'' which has been predicted by prior contact survey and synthetic population studies.\autocite{mossongSocialContactsMixing2008,premProjectingContactMatrices2021,mistryInferringHighresolutionHuman2021}
This highlights the important contribution of household transmission.
Further work is necessary to determine if the directionality of transmission (adult to child versus child to adult) can be determined based on existing data (such as the sequence collection dates), but this will be challenging without paired household data.
We also saw some evidence for a potential grandparent-infant relationship with 0-year-olds having multiple $RR_{seq}$ > 1 with ages 70-80 years old.
However, age 0 was undersampled (n = 5,129 sequences) compared to neighboring age groups (ages 1-3 ranged from n= 12,388 to 15,026) and other toddler ages did not show a similar intergenerational pattern with elderly adults.
Furthermore, age 0 could have been used as a placeholder value for unknown age by certain submitting groups.
Therefore, some caution is necessary in interpreting its relationship to other ages.
Additionally, we were concerned that the same-age $RR_{seq}$ may be over-estimated due to repeat testing from the same individual.
We are limited in our ability to control for this, because unique person identifiers are not included in the data.
Instead, we used identical sequence pairs with the same age, geographical unit information, and gender (if present) that occurred within a 3-day window as a marker for the same individual and removed these pairs from the RR calculation.
To determine the optimal window length, we conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing same age $RR_{seq}$ for same gender versus different gender pairs (Supplemental Fig.\ \ref{fig:age_gender}).
We found the 3-day window reduced same age $RR_{seq}$ inflation for pediatric and young adult groups (though inflation persisted for older ages).
Overall, the age identical sequence matrix was correlated with the synthetic age contact matrices, validating their utility in predicting transmission patterns.\autocite{mistryInferringHighresolutionHuman2021}
Our age analysis also highlighted that age structure was more important for within subdivision transmission and that age transmission matrix regressed towards $RR = 1$ as the geographic scale expanded.
This suggests that when modeling outbreaks, age structure can be simplified/omitted for transmission across large scales, but high resolution matrices are important for the local dynamics.

We used normalized $RR_{seq}$ to identify changes in transmission patterns over time, including describing how transmission changed across geographic scales over the course of the pandemic.
We found a sequential increase in transmission across from intra-regional to international subdivision pairs (Fig.\ \ref{fig:geo_time}A-B) from 2020 to 2022, suggesting that transmission patterns followed a graduated restoring of movement and travel patterns.
This likely was a response to multiple factors including loosening of travel restrictions, perceived immunity after vaccination/natural infection, and decreased perception of COVID-19's severity.
Additionally, we explored whether this normalized $RR_{seq}$ could help characterize changes in the transmission of the elderly after the vaccine rollout in January 2021 and transmission patterns of school age children throughout the pandemic (Supplemental Fig.\ \ref{fig:age_time}).
Both of these analyses looking at changes in transmission across ages were able to detect changes, but their interpretation is somewhat more challenging as host immune profiles (including vaccination history), social patterns of interaction and schooling/work behaviors were changing through much of the time periods of interest.

With an analysis of such a large and diverse set of viral genomes, there are some important limitations to consider when interpreting these results.
First, while the RR calculation adjusts for the number of sequences sampled in each deme, oversampling specific populations can still bias the results.
For example, we found in our geographic analysis (Fig.\ \ref{fig:geo_clust}) that Oklahoma had the highest $RR_{seq}$ with Mexico of any US state, despite it not being a direct border state.
We further investigated Oklahoma's sequencing effort compared to other subdivisions with $RR_{seq} > 1$ with Mexico (Supplemental Fig.\ \ref{fig:ok_epiweek}), and found that Oklahoma had a disproportionate number of sequences during the Omicron BA.1 wave from December 2021 to March 2022.
This time period corresponded with an acute drop in genomic diversity as BA.1 swept the population.\autocite{lythgoeLineageReplacementEvolution2023}
With Oklahoma's sequences concentrated during this sweep, its relative number of identical sequences is inflated compared to other subdivisions.
This example highlights the importance of assessing sampling heterogeneity with this method, especially when calculating $RR_{seq}$ over larger time periods where sequencing effort may be variable.
Additionally, while we have extended the identical sequence RR approach to look for temporal associations, we would be cautious in trying to identify any causal explanations from our results.
Our $RR_{seq}$ scores were calculated from observational data as multiple host, environmental, and viral factors were rapidly changing.
As a result, it is difficult to use these data alone to generate appropriate counterfactuals for causal inference.
Even with these limitations, we showed that the $RR_{seq}$ metric can characterize rich transmission patterns across a large geographic area.
Furthermore, with appropriate sampling design and supporting data, this framework could be implemented prospectively to test how public health interventions affect transmission networks.
The results of this work highlight the importance of continued pathogen genome surveillance by public health organizations, as it can be used to improve our understanding of infectious disease epidemiology.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data Sources}
We used all the 5,824,505 SARS-CoV-2 genomes available in the EpiCoV database on GISAID for Canada, Mexico, and the United States of America from January 2020 through December 2024.\autocite{elbeDataDiseaseDiplomacy2017,shuGISAIDGlobalInitiative2017,khareGISAIDsRolePandemic2021}
The EPI\_SET with the full list of the genome accession numbers is accessible at \url{https://doi.org/10.55876/gis8.260827fq}.
Consensus sequences were extracted from the GISAID EpiCoV database and curated using the Nextstrain nCoV ingest pipeline,\autocite{hadfieldNextstrainRealtimeTracking2018} with Pango lineages and sequence coverage assigned by Nextclade.\autocite{aksamentovNextcladeCladeAssignment2021}
We limited genomes to be those from human hosts with a valid collection date and a minimum of 90\% sequencing coverage.
Additionally, for sequences from the United States and Canada, we restricted our analysis to entries with division metadata corresponding to a valid US state or Canadian province.
We excluded genomes from US territories other than the District of Columbia from our analysis due to their small sample size.
After applying these filters, we obtained a SARS-CoV-2 sequence dataset composed of 5,765,925 genomes, which we used in subsequent analyses.
We restricted our age analysis to sequences with valid single age metadata (defined as a numeric positive value less than 125 years), with ages 99 and older aggregated into a single age category.
Geographic distance between US states was defined as the minimum distance between their core-based statistical areas (CBSAs).\autocite{bureauMetropolitanMicropolitanStatistical}
Travel and movement variables were adapted into relative risk (RR) scores by using the same formula as the identical sequence RR calculation, substituting the number of shared trips for the number of sequence pairs.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}
We calculated ground travel RR between states using the 2017 National Household Travel Survey's day trip file, air travel RR from the 2019 Airline Origin and Destination Survey (DB1B), and smartphone mobility RR from timeshare data collected by SafeGraph.\autocite{NationalHouseholdTravel,DB1B_Dataset,pullanoCharacterizingUSSpatial2025}
Finally, we used synthetic contact matrices based on survey data to compare against the patterns inferred from $RR_{seq}$ in the US and Canada.\autocite{mistryInferringHighresolutionHuman2021}

\subsection{SARS-CoV-2 Identical Sequence Identification}
We identified pairs of identical sequences by extending the pipeline from our previous work in Washington State.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}
First, we computed a genetic pairwise distance matrix between sequences in our dataset.
To minimize unnecessary comparisons, we only calculated distances for sequences within the same Pango lineage, and used \texttt{pairsnp} to find all pairs of sequences with a Hamming distance equal to 0.\autocite{aksamentovNextcladeCladeAssignment2021,tonkin-hillGtonkinhillPairsnp2025}
We removed sequences found in over 1,000 distinct identical pairs from the analysis as they biased the RR calculations towards neutrality ($RR_{seq} = 1$).
These sequences typically had missing bases at sites with key mutations, which therefore inflated the number of matching identical sequences.
We also removed pairs of identical sequences that had the same host metadata (age, gender, division/location) and that were collected within 3 days of each other, due to concern that they could represent repeat sampling of the same individual.
We then stored the final sequence metadata and list of identical pairs in a DuckDB relational database, which allowed for fast operations without loading the full list into system memory.
This database was accessed within the R programming environment using the \texttt{dbplyr} library.\autocite{rcoreteamLanguageEnvironmentStatistical2025,wickhamDbplyrDplyrBack2025}

\subsection{Identical Sequence Relative Risk Calculation}
We used our previously described identical sequence relative risk calculation to characterize SARS-CoV-2 transmission between different geographic units and age groups.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}
We defined $n_{A,B}$ to be the number of pairs of genomes with a Hamming distance of 0 where one sequence belongs to group $A$ and the other sequence belongs to group $B$.
We then defined $n_{A,\bullet}= \sum_{\forall B}n_{A,B}$ (i.e. all pairs of identical sequences where at least one sequence is in group $A$) and $n_{\bullet,\bullet} = \sum_{\forall A}n_{A,\bullet}$ (i.e. the total number of pairs of identical sequences).
We calculated the $RR_{seq}^{A,B}$ as follows:

\[RR_{seq}^{A,B} = \frac{n_{A,B}/n_{A,\bullet}}{n_{B,\bullet}/n_{\bullet,\bullet}}\]

We calculated $RR_{seq}$ scores between North American subdivisions and single-year age groups through the binned 99+ age group.
Due to noisier estimates in higher age groups with decreased sequence counts, we focused on presenting ages 0--80 in the main text, but the RR matrix stored in our repository contains the full age range.
Additionally, we were able to use this approach to subset the population and look at the interaction between multiple variables at the same time (e.g. the $RR_{seq}$ of individuals with the same age within the same subdivision compared to the $RR_{seq}$ of individuals with the same age not in the same subdivision).
We calculated uncertainty through subsampling 80\% of the total sequences without replacement to create a 95\% confidence interval.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}

In prior work, we showed that the magnitude of $RR_{seq}$ is impacted by transmission intensity, because a faster spreading pathogen results in larger clusters of identical sequences which dilutes the epidemiological signal available in identical pairs.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}
This makes the raw $RR_{seq}$ metric unsuitable to evaluate temporal changes in transmission structure.
Here, we extended $RR_{seq}$ to describe changes over time.
First, we defined $RR_{seq}^{A,B}(t)$ to be the $RR_{seq}^{A,B}$ for pairs whose collection dates fall between $t$ and $t + \Delta t$, with $\Delta t$ being some fixed interval width.
Changes in $RR_{seq}$ can be due to true changes in the transmission pattern between demes $A$ and $B$, but they can also reflect changes in the baseline force of infection of either the pathogen (e.g. variants having differences in pathogenicity) or the host populations (e.g. behavioral/movement changes, immunizations).
To account for this, we computed a normalized version of the $RR_{seq}$ against a baseline population.

For time analyses of geographic $RR_{seq}$, changes in the circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants, public health interventions, and sequence sampling effort over time could affect the number of genomes collected from a specific area.
Additionally, no single geographic area serves as a natural reference population in this case.
As the focus of this method was to determine if we could measure how much mixing changed between populations over time, we used the average of the within subdivision $RR_{seq}$ for the two subdivisions as the normalizing factor.
This can be written as follows:
\begin{align*}
nRR_{seq}^{A,B}(t) &= \frac{RR_{seq}^{A,B}(t)}{\frac{1}{2}(RR_{seq}^{A,A}(t)+RR_{seq}^{B,B}(t))} \\
             &= \frac{2 RR_{seq}^{A,B}(t)}{RR_{seq}^{A,A}(t)+RR_{seq}^{B,B}(t)}
\end{align*}
It is important to note that with this normalization, there is not a clear scale that indicates whether a subdivision pairing has enrichment of identical sequences.
Since $RR_{seq}^{A,A}$ and $RR_{seq}^{B,B}$ tend to be greater than $RR_{seq}^{A,B}$, we expected $nRR_{seq}^{A,B} < 1$.
Instead, we focused on its utility to look at changes over time and comparisons against other across-subdivision pairings.
To validate this approach, we used agent-based modeling to simulate the spread of infection with corresponding nucleotide mutations.
This included creating scenarios where the transmission network was modified between geographic units to emulate the effects of a transportation shutdown.
Further discussion of these validation simulations can be found in the supplementary information, and results are shown in Supplemental Fig.\ \ref{fig:sim_results}.

For time analyses of the age $RR_{seq}$, our objective was to detect changes in transmission across age groups due to changes in interactions between the groups (e.g. school closures, movement restrictions) or if one age group acquired immunity (e.g. vaccination).
Prior studies have shown that during the initial phases of the COVID-19 pandemic, young adults (i.e. 18--40-year-olds) tended to have lower levels of social isolation and risk mitigation behaviors compared to other age groups \autocite{kimHowDoesAge2020,helenaj.hutchinsCOVID19MitigationBehaviors2020,engeliSocialContactPatterns} and disproportionately contribute to transmission \autocite{monodAgeGroupsThat2021,trankiemSARSCoV2TransmissionAge2021}.
Therefore, we used working-age adults as a reference group (designated here as $Y$) for normalization.
Age $RR_{seq}$ was normalized over time using the following equation: $nRR_{seq}^{A,B}(t) = RR_{seq}^{A,B}(t)/RR_{seq}^{Y,Y}(t)$, with $nRR_{seq}^{Y,Y}(t) = 1$ for all $t$.

\subsection{Geographic Clustering and Regional Analysis}

We used hierarchical clustering and dimensionality reduction to assess how spatial transmission patterns between North American subdivisions related with administrative boundary definitions.
$RR_{seq}^{A,B}$ was converted to a dissimilarity score between $A$ and $B$: $\exp{(-RR_{seq}^{A,B})}$, which we used as an input distance matrix for clustering and dimensionality reduction analysis.
We performed hierarchical clustering of North American subdivisions the agglomerative nesting (AGNES) algorithm from the \texttt{cluster} R package.\autocite{maechlerClusterClusterAnalysis2026}
We conducted principal coordinate analysis (i.e. classical multidimensional scaling) with the \texttt{cmdscale} function from the base \texttt{stats} R package\autocite{rcoreteamLanguageEnvironmentStatistical2025} and used the absolute eigenvalue proportion for estimating the percentage of variance in the genetic data explained by each principal coordinate.
Given that identical sequence enrichment was often concentrated within regions or with immediate neighboring subdivisions, we sought to identify cross-region pairs of non-neighboring North American subdivisions with persistently high $RR_{seq}$ over time to find corridors of cross-region transmission.
Since the geographic $nRR_{seq}$ does not have a clear threshold for enrichment, we ranked $nRR_{seq}$ across all non-neighboring subdivision pairs based on percentile values, and then restricted this ranking to pairs belonging to distinct regions.
All international pairs were eligible regardless of neighbor status.
Pairs ranked in the 98th percentile or higher during at least 5 quarters were defined as persistent cross-region connections.
Additionally, we defined pairs of subdivisions as having a persistent seasonal connection if their normalized $RR_{seq}$ ranked in the 98th percentile in at least 2 years over our study period.
For example, Florida and California had normalized $RR_{seq}$ higher than the 98th percentile during the July--September quarters of 2022, 2023, and 2024, leading to them being identified as a persistent seasonal connection for Q3.

We calculated Pearson's correlation coefficients between identical sequence RR and these distance/movement, where all RR variables (both $RR_{seq}$ and travel/movement) were first transformed to a base-10 logarithmic scale.
We restricted this comparison to pairs of different states with reported travel or movement.

\subsection{Description of the computational workflow implemented to calculate identical sequence relative risk metrics}
In this paragraph, we describe the workflow we implemented to compute identical sequence relative risk scores from 5.7 million North American SARS-CoV-2 sequences.
This involved the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Generating a full list of pairs of identical sequences
    \item Computing RR scores between pairs of demes from this identical sequence pair list
\end{enumerate}

To generate a full list of pairs of identical sequences, we used \texttt{pairSNP}.\autocite{tonkin-hillGtonkinhillPairsnp2025}
Calculating the distance matrix between all sequences would have resulted in $\sim$16.6 trillion comparisons.
To reduce the necessary number of comparisons, we only calculated the distance matrix for sequences within the same Pango lineage,\autocite{aksamentovNextcladeCladeAssignment2021} and we limited \texttt{pairSNP} outputs to only return zero distance pairs using the \texttt{-d 0} option.
Using a 32 core Xeon server with 160 GB RAM allocated, subsetting the metadata and sequence data by Pango lineage, calculating lineage-specific distance matrices, and combining the matrices to form the final identical pair list took 36--48 hours to run.
We then excluded any sequences that had over 1000 identical pairs as outliers, based on sensitivity analyses of their effect on relative risk calculation and the distribution of identical pairs per sequence.
Additionally, we cleaned the metadata as described in the Methods section and removed any sequences with invalid geographical metadata or that did not meet our QC thresholds.

For the relative risk calculation, our goal was to compute $RR_{seq}$ for each set of demes of interest on our local machines.
While the calculation of a relative risk is a series of simple arithmetic operations, counting the number of pairs within demes and annotating the pairs with pertinent metadata is challenging due to the size of the datasets (4.0 GB for the list of sequence pairs and 7.7 GB for the metadata dataframe).
Loading both of these files into an R session's memory can strain a typical desktop or laptop machine to the limits of its memory and reduce performance, especially for analyses requiring multiple subsamples such as our uncertainty calculation.
Therefore, we combined these files into a portable SQL database and used DuckDB via the \texttt{dbplyr} R package to complete all the queries needed for the RR calculation.
The resulting database was smaller than either file (1.5 GB) and RR calculation performance was excellent.
On an M1 Macbook Pro with 16 GB of RAM, calculating the full age RR matrix takes 7--8 seconds for the single year age bins and the geographic RR matrix takes 11--12 seconds for the subdivision bins.
This allowed us to quickly perform any post hoc analyses on our local machines as DuckDB automatically implements parallel processing to accelerate analyses.
The portable database also allows for easy sharing of data between multiple team members.
The main drawback of the SQL database arises when creating bootstrap samples for RR confidence intervals, as there is some performance overhead for DuckDB + \texttt{dbplyr} queries compared to running the command directly in R.
For analyses where only a subset of the total sequences are necessary for RR calculation (such as the time-based analyses), we can use a hybrid approach of pulling the subset from DuckDB into R's memory and then doing the sampling and analysis within R.

\subsection{Simulation study to evaluate the performance of the normalized relative risk approach}

In this paragraph, we describe our simulation study to validate that the normalized $RR_{seq}$ metric can capture changes in transmission patterns.
The code used to run these simulations is accessible in a separate GitHub repository: \url{https://github.com/abemanian/ncov-sim-mig}
We used the \texttt{nosoi} package\autocite{lequimeNosoiStochasticAgentbased2020} to conduct an agent-based simulation which accounts for movement across demes and generates a transmission tree.
The full list of simulation parameters is shown in Supplemental Table \ref{tab:sim_params}, and basic infection characteristics (e.g. incubation, infection period) were selected to mimic a SARS-CoV-2-like outbreak.
For each day of the outbreak, an agent undergoes three phases: (1) movement, (2) contact and (3) transmission.
We simulated outbreaks in a synthetic system of 6 geographic units as shown in Figure \ref{fig:sim_network}.
The movement structure between these 6 units is split between two clusters each with 3 units: $\{A, B, C\}$ and $\{X, Y, Z\}$ with the only bridge across the regions being $A-X$.
Agents across all units had a baseline movement probability of $0.10$ for each time step and then randomly moved within the network according to the edge probabilities.
Next the agent has a number of contacts assigned to it drawn from a negative binomial distribution.
Then, we check if the agent has been infected beyond an incubation period (drawn for each agent from a Gamma distribution) before it can proceed to infect its contact.
The per-contact transmission probability is drawn independently for each agent at each day from a Beta distribution.
The transmission probability was then suppressed logistically based on local prevalence by a factor of $1/(1+e^{(n-n_{1/2})/s})$ with $n$ being the number of currently infected hosts in each subdivision.
This scale factor helps limit transmission to a steady state where the effective reproductive number ($R_{t}$, defined as mean number of offspring cases per infection) reaches an equilibrium of $1$ (shown in Supplemental Fig.\ \ref{fig:rt_combined}).
Agents remained infectious based on a Bernoulli trial at the start of each day, resulting in a geometric distribution of infectious durations.
For all scenarios, the outbreak was seeded with a single infected case in deme ${A}$ and only simulations with successful sustained transmission (defined as a minimum of 200 cases and 100 cases before time 100) were included in our analysis.

We explored multiple movement reduction scenarios to test if $nRR_{seq}$ could detect differences.
To simulate a movement shutdown scenario, we reduced the daily movement probability down to 10\% of the original movement rate (i.e. a 90\% reduction) at time 100.
We then assumed a progressive movement recovery, with movement increasing every 50 days at 150 (to 33\%), 200 (to 66\%), and 250 (to 100\% of the original rate).
In order to evaluate whether the normalized relative risk measure could detect changes in the movement network, we considered an alternative scenario (``staggered recovery'') where the bridge between units A and X had a further delayed recovery (10\% at 150, 33\% at 200, and 50\% at 250) while all the within-region connections had the same recovery as the ``uniform'' scenario.
All scenarios ended at time 350 days.
We compared these against a scenario with no movement reduction.
For each of these scenarios, we simulated 25 synthetic outbreaks.
From each simulation, we obtained a Newick transmission tree which was input into \texttt{phastSim} to simulate nucleotide sequence evolution along the tree with a fixed molecular clock rate.\autocite{demaioPhastSimEfficientSimulation2022}
We used the Wuhan-Hu-1 reference genome as the sequence of the index case (i.e. the transmission tree root) and a substitution rate of $2 \times 10^{-6}$ substitutions per site per day.

We subsampled the simulated sequences by time for a total of 8000 sequences per simulation.
Subsampling was stratified by 10-day time windows to ensure there was an even number of sampled sequences for each window, as uniform sampling would not capture a sufficient number of sequences in the early phase of the outbreak.
Sampling was neutral across all subdivisions so deme composition is unbiased.
We then calculated $RR_{seq}$ and $nRR_{seq}$ between each geographic unit pair.
Identical sequence pairs were assigned to a phase if both sequences occurred in that phase's time window (see Supplemental Table \ref{tab:sim_phases} for phase definitions), with pairs spanning across phases excluded.
$nRR_{seq}$ across subdivisions was normalized against the mean of the within subdivision $nRR_{seq}$ (i.e. same as the definition used for the geographic analyses in the main text).
We use the $nRR_{seq}$ of infection pairs immediately around the shutdown phase (i.e. day 70 to day 110) as a baseline $nRR_{seq}$ to assess if transmission returns to its pre-shutdown level.
Results of this analysis are shown in Figure \ref{fig:sim_results}.
We found that the $nRR_{seq}$ was able to describe temporal changes in transmission structure over time.
For example, in the scenario with no movement shutdown, we did not find meaningful changes in $nRR_{seq}$ over time.
However, in both shutdown scenarios, we found the $nRR_{seq}$ decreased in both within-region and cross-region pairings with cross-region pairings having a greater drop.
In the staggered recovery scenario, we recovered that the cross-region $nRR_{seq}$ had a slower recovery than within-region $nRR_{seq}$ and did not fully reach pre-shutdown levels.

\subsection{Air Travel Definition: DB1B vs T100}
We investigated using the US Department of Transportation's T-100 market dataset versus the DB1B dataset to estimate air travel flows.
The T-100 set measures each travel leg volumes instead of looking at flows between boarding origin and destinations.
It is not possible to tell which passengers arrive at their final destination versus a layover between flights, making interpretation of flows difficult.
While the raw correlation between T-100 and identical sequence RRs across all state pairs was higher than for the DB1B dataset ($r$ = 0.28 vs 0.12), we ultimately felt this approach misclassified flows as many trips between non-hub cities will require a layover at a regional hub first.
When looking for correlation across our travel/distance variables (Fig.\ \ref{fig:pred_corr}) we found that the T-100 RR scores tend to be more correlated with NHTS ground travel data and CBSA distance than with the DB1B ticket data.
Furthermore, the partial correlation of the T-100 market data with identical sequence RR after controlling for distance is weaker than the DB1B partial correlation (0.108 vs 0.222).
These findings suggest that using T-100 segment leg flows captures much of the same identical sequence RR variance as distance and adds less new information compared to the DB1B data.

\subsection{Additional Distance/Movement Interaction Analyses}

To quantify the unique contribution of each movement metric beyond geographic distance, we computed Pearson's correlations between $\log_{10}(RR_{seq})$ and each $\log_{10}$-transformed travel/mobility RR.
We found both the marginal correlation the partial correlation adjusting for CBSA distance (Table~\ref{tab:partial_corr}).
Partial correlations were obtained by correlating the residuals of each variable after regression on a thin-plate spline smooth of distance $s(\text{CBSA dist.})$, which accounts for the strongly nonlinear effect of distance.
Once distance is accounted for, smartphone mobility retains essentially no independent association ($r=0.024$), while ground travel (NHTS) and origin--destination air travel (DB1B) retain the largest unique signal.
Band-stratified correlations (Table~\ref{tab:band_corr}) show that the movement-transmission association is concentrated at short range for smartphone mobility and at long range for air travel.

The significance of the distance $\times$ movement interaction was assessed by likelihood-ratio-style tests comparing an additive tensor-spline model (i.e. $\text{ti(movement)} + \text{ti(CBSA dist.)}$) against a model with an additional interaction term (i.e. $\text{ti(movement, CBSA dist.)}$), fit by maximum likelihood with the \texttt{mgcv} package (Table~\ref{tab:interaction_lrt}).
The interaction term is highly significant for every movement metric in both the negative-binomial count and Gaussian $\log_{10}(\text{RR})$ formulations of the models.

\subsection{Changes in Age-Based Identical Sequence RR Over Time}

We investigate whether temporal changes in age-based $RR_{seq}$ can be used to detect transmission flow changes in response to public health interventions.
First we examine whether the transmission patterns for elderly adults changed during the initial COVID-19 vaccine rollout period (Winter 2020 through Spring 2021), shown in Supplemental Fig. \ref{fig:age_time}A.
For all three countries, vaccine rollout was initially prioritized to individuals with medical comorbidities, older age, and residence in a long-term care (LTC) facility.
Therefore, we focus on the $nRR_{seq}$ for the 65--80 and 80+ age groups across rolling 2-month windows from November 2020 to May 2021, using the within-group $RR_{seq}$ of 25--64-year-olds as the reference category.
We find that for the 65--80 group (left panel) transmission was highest with the 65--80 and 80+ groups, as would be expected from our age heatmap (Fig. \ref{fig:age}A).
Notably there are minimal differences between these two curves across all months, and both show a transient increase from 1.3--1.4 in November 2020 to a peak of 1.7--1.8 in late February.
For the 80+ group (right panel), within-group transmission is substantially higher than transmission between the 65--80 and 80+ groups, with the within-group $nRR_{seq}$ being 2--4 fold higher.
The 80+ within-group $nRR_{seq}$ dropped from 6.4 in November 2020 to 3.5 in January 2021 and then fluctuated between roughly 3.5 and 5 for the remainder of the period.
All three countries introduced the COVID-19 vaccine in December 2020, consistent with the timing of the decrease in 80+ transmission.
However, we do not see corresponding transmission structure changes in the 65--80 group, and the decrease in the 80+ group is transient.
The significance of this drop is difficult to ascertain due to the wider confidence intervals for the 80+/80+ $RR_{seq}$ during this time period.
The lack of a drop in the 65--80 curves may be because much of this group was vaccinated on a similar schedule to the broader adult population, whereas the 80+ group was vaccinated earlier.
Furthermore, the 80+ age category has the highest proportion of LTC residents of any age group, which would have increased its vaccination priority and helped with rapid vaccine deployment in this population.
These $nRR_{seq}$ curves may also be affected by non-vaccination changes in transmission, such as increased social isolation and differential rates of natural infection and immunity across populations.
Notably, the $nRR_{seq}$ only measures relative changes in transmission and cannot be used to determine absolute changes in incidence.
Ultimately, we find an age-specific change in transmission within the 80+ group that may have been tied to the vaccine rollout, but the signal is not definitive as it is isolated to a single age group pairing and transient.

We also investigate the dynamics of transmission patterns among school-age children (ages 5--11 and 12--17) over the course of the pandemic.
Schooling transitioned to virtual and hybrid models for many states and provinces across all three countries starting from spring 2020 and throughout the 2020--2021 school year.\autocite{COVID19ProvincialPolicies, DataResourcesCOVID19, boruchowiczTimeUseYouth2022}
Therefore, we assess whether within group transmission for school-age children decreased during these time periods.
$nRR_{seq}$ are calculated in fixed 3-month windows corresponding to seasons: Winter (December to February), Spring (March to May), Summer (June to August), and Autumn (September to November), with the results shown in Supplemental Fig. \ref{fig:age_time}B.
We limit our analysis specifically to same-subdivision pairs to focus on the role of local transmission (e.g. within school and household).
For both school-age groups we find transmission to be generally higher with other pediatric age groups (0--4, 5--11, 12--17) than with the adult age groups throughout the time course of the pandemic, and the within-group $nRR_{seq}$ curves are the highest.
The earliest pandemic time points (March through August 2020) are calculated based on very few within-subdivision pediatric pairs (fewer than 100 per season) and produce extremely noisy nRR estimates that are difficult to interpret.
From Fall 2020 onward, the pediatric $nRR_{seq}$ curves were suppressed throughout the 2020--2021 school year, with the within-group $nRR_{seq}$ for both school-age groups around 1.5 by spring 2021 and the between-pediatric-group $nRR_{seq}$ (e.g. 12--17 with 0--4) regressing to the working-age baseline ($nRR_{seq} \approx 1$).
The within-group curves then rose as in-person schooling became the dominant modality in the 2021--2022 school year and continued to rise through the 2022--2023 school year.
After summer 2023, the $nRR_{seq}$ estimates became increasingly noisy again as pair counts decreased (down to tens to hundreds of pairs), which corresponds to the increasing width of the confidence intervals around this time.
In summary, we are able to find some evidence of decreased school-age transmission for the 2020--2021 compared to the 2021--2022 and 2022--2023 school years, suggesting that school closures may have reduced the force of transmission between children.
However, in-person schooling policies were highly variable across the geographies which limits our ability to draw any causal relationship between these policies and the change in transmission patterns.
Additionally, if immunity was higher in the older populations due to vaccination and earlier natural infection, this would have increased the relative susceptibility of younger age groups and caused an apparent increased force of infection.
