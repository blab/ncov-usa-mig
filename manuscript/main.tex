\section{Introduction}

Genomic surveillance has become a fundamental tool for understanding pathogen spread and evolution.
In particular, the COVID-19 pandemic has led to the sequencing of SARS-CoV-2 at an unprecedented rate, with 16.7 million genomes submitted to GISAID's EpiCoV database from December 2019 through December 2024.\autocite{khareGISAIDsRolePandemic2021}
Data at this scale should theoretically make it possible to study transmission in incredible detail.
Phylodynamics methods leveraging such sequence data typically rely on ancestral tree reconstruction to recover transmission history and population-level mixing patterns.\autocite{dengGenomicSurveillanceReveals2020,neherNextfluRealtimeTracking2015}
These tree-based approaches are powerful, but they scale poorly because the number of possible phylogenies increases factorially with the number of genomes, limiting analyses to thousands or tens of thousands of sequences.
The number of sequences that can be included in an analysis limits the resolution at which transmission can be studied.
Characterizing spread patterns between $n$ population groups indeed involves inferring transmission between $\sim n^2$ pairs of groups, and sequence data requirements become substantial as the number of groups increase.
Overcoming these limitations would thus make it possible to study spread across many fine-grained groups and better understand complex transmission structure.

Statistical methods that identify structure across genomes without building phylogenies \autocite{mattesonGenomicSurveillanceReveals2023,sobkowiakCov2clustersGenomicClustering2022,cahuantziUnsupervisedIdentificationSignificant2024,nanduriDimensionalityReductionDistills2024} offer a promising avenue. 
In prior work, we demonstrated that the composition of clusters of identical sequences is informative about transmission between population groups.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}
Because pathogens accumulate mutations along transmission chains, identical sequences tend to capture epidemiologically linked individuals, and the rate at which two groups share identical sequences reflects the intensity of transmission between them.
We introduced an identical sequence relative risk ($RR_{seq}$) metric that measures how the rate at which identical sequences are observed between two groups compares with what is expected under random mixing while accounting for sequencing effort.
Explicitly accounting for sequencing effort also enables to fully leverage existing genome datasets, and understand the role all groups, including less sampled ones, in transmission.
We used this framework to map county-level geographic patterns of SARS-CoV-2 spread in Washington State from over 100,000 sequences.
Given this approach only relies on genetic distance calculations, it has the potential to scale to even larger datasets and reconstruct transmission patterns at high resolution.

Here, we analyse patterns of occurrence of identical sequences among 5.7 million publicly available SARS-CoV-2 genomes from North America (Canada, Mexico, and the United States (US)) collected between January 2020 and December 2024.
We investigate transmission between 62 North American subdivisions, at the level of US states and Canadian provinces.
This granularity enables us to characterize transmission structure comprehensively across a continent through time, and to understand how it relates to administrative boundaries typically used for aggregation.
We also investigate how different types of mobility indicators recapitulate transmission at different spatial scales.
Finally, we characterize transmission between age groups at single-year resolution, a level of detailed not previously achieved from either epidemiological or genomic data, and difficult to reach with methods relying on more divergent genomes.\autocite{tran-kiemCharacterizingInformativenessPathogen2026}
The combination of extremely rich sequence data and our highly scalable method enables a rich analysis that finds nuanced relationships that would have been obscured by subsampling or aggregating the units of analyses.

Between January 2020 and December 2024, 6,187,049 SARS-CoV-2 sequences were deposited in the GISAID EpiCoV database across Canada, the US and Mexico (hereafter referred to as North America).\autocite{khareGISAIDsRolePandemic2021}
We defined North American subdivisions as states in the US (including the District of Columbia but excluding all other territories), provinces in Canada and the country of Mexico based on the available geographic resolution in the sequence metadata for the 3 countries.
Among these sequences, 5,765,925 had valid subdivision metadata and coverage greater than 90\%.
Figure \ref{fig:desc}A displays the geographic distribution of sequences by North American subdivisions.
We find considerable heterogeneity in sequence volume by subdivision.
California has the highest number of sequences available with 935,874 sequences and Prince Edward Island has the lowest with 1,148 sequences.
When adjusting for population size, sequencing effort still varied across subdivision with a median of 1468.7 sequences per 100,000 persons (IQR: 934.9 - 2288.9)
Wyoming had the highest per capita sequence count of any subdivision (5,935.5 sequences per 100,000 persons) while Mexico had the lowest number of sequences per capita of any subdivision (76.5 sequences per 100,000 persons).
Looking across countries, all of them had similar sequence count trajectories, with the number of sequences per capita increasing in early 2021 and peaking in late 2021/early 2022 following the Delta and Omicron waves (Figure \ref{fig:desc}B) and sequencing intensity decreasing in all three countries by 2024.
Mexico consistently had the fewest number of sequences per capita of the three countries (Figure \ref{fig:desc}B).
Among the 5.7 million of sequences available in North America, 52.0\% have valid age metadata. The full age distribution shown in Figure \ref{fig:desc}C) and stratified across regions in Figure \ref{fig:desc}D.
Publicly available SARS-CoV-2 sequences in North America thus represent a particularly rich dataset with detailed metadata, which enables us to conduct an in-depth analysis of transmission patterns across ages, geography, and time.

We calculate $RR_{seq}$ scores between all North American subdivisions to identify spatial patterns of transmission.
Figure \ref{fig:geo_clust}A shows chloropleth maps that illustrate patterns for a few focal subdivisions (Ontario province, New York state, and Mexico).
In all three examples, $RR_{seq}$ values are highest within the focal subdivision and $RR_{seq}$ tends to decay with distance, highlighting that transmission increases with geographic proximity.
Despite New York state and Ontario being adjacent, we do not find evidence for enriched transmission between these two subdivisions ($RR_{seq}^{NY,ON} = 0.396 < 1$).
By contrast, we find $RR_{seq}$ scores greater than 1 (indicating enrichment) between Mexico and several US states including California, Texas, New Mexico, and Oklahoma.
To evaluate whether this difference in cross border pattern could be an artifact from not being able to split Mexico into subdivisions, we perform a sensitivity analysis aggregating Canada into a single unit and find $RR_{seq} < 1$ across all states (max $RR_{seq}$ of 0.26).
This suggests that transmission is not fully described by proximity alone and that administrative (here national) boundaries may further shape transmission risk.
Oklahoma-Mexico was a significant outlier relationship with a $RR_{seq} = 4.13$ (bootstrap 95\% CI: 3.86-4.54) compared to the other enriched US-Mexico relationships ($RR_{seq}$ ranging from 1.09-1.37).
Further investigation found that a disproportionate number of Oklahoma sequences were from the Omicron BA.1 wave (45.0\%) compared to California, Texas, and Mexico (17.9\%-22.0\%), which likely would have decreased the Oklahoma's captured genetic diversity and inflated its enrichment with Mexico.
In-depth results of this analysis are shown in supplemental figure \ref{fig:ba1_epiweek}.

To further characterize geographic structure in SARS-CoV-2 transmission in North America, we perform hierarchical clustering using a genetic distance measure based on $RR_{seq}$ values between subdivisions.
Figure \ref{fig:geo_clust}B represents a heatmap of pairwise $RR_{seq}$ between subdivisions with rows and columns ordered based on hierarchical clustering results.
Rows are further annotated based on geographic regions defined in figure \ref{fig:geo_pc}A.
We identify a block structure along the diagonal, where blocks correspond to groups of subdivisions with elevated rate of identical sequence occurrence.
These blocks can be interpreted as relevant transmission units for SARS-CoV-2 and generally match existing geographic regions.
For example, Eastern Canada, Western Canada, Mideast, and Plains regions had all of their subdivisions sit on contiguous branches of the hierarchical cluster tree.
By contrast, a few US regions did not perfectly sort along regional definitions.
For example, states from the Southeast do not form a single transmission unit and are split into different smaller clusters (shown in depth in supplemental figure \ref{fig:hclust_subset}):
(1) the District of Columbia (DC), Maryland, Virginia, and North Carolina cluster with the Mideast and New England states;
(2) Arkansas, Louisiana, and Mississippi group with Southwestern states and Mexico;
and (3) the remaining states cluster with states from the Great Lakes area.
We observe a similar pattern in the Far West, with Alaska, Oregon and Washington displaying greater transmission link with Idaho and Utah (Rocky Mountain), while California, Hawaii, and Nevada rather cluster with Arizona and Texas

To investigate whether transmission patterns reflect continental geographic structure, we conduct dimensionality reduction using principal coordinate analysis (fig \ref{fig:geo_pc}B), using the same dissimilarity matrix as the hierarchical clustering.
The first principal coordinate (PC1) cleanly partitions Canadian provinces from US states, with Mexico close to the US cloud but well separated from the Canadian provinces.
The second principal coordinate (PC2) corresponds broadly to an East-West axis.
Although these first two coordinates map well onto geographic structure, they only capture $\sim$30\% of the total variance in identical sequence occurrence patterns.
This indicates that, while geographic proximity between subdivisions is an important determinant of transmission risk, it is insufficient to explain the full structure of transmission.

    \label{fig:geo_time}

In order to investigate temporal relationships across states and provinces, we calculate quarterly $RR_{seq}$ scores normalized by the average of within subdivision $RR_{seq}$ scores.
Normalized $RR_{seq}$ ($nRR_{seq}$) were highly variable in Q1 2020 and rapidly decreased to a minimum in 2020 Q4 (fig \ref{fig:geo_time}A), indicating a global reduction of across subdivision transmission.
Across subdivision transmission slowly increased during 2021 and reached a peak during 2022.
Throughout all time periods, subdivision pairs within the same region had higher identical sequence transmission compared to between region and international pairs.
Fold-change of $nRR_{seq}$ are also calculated with respect to 2023 Q1 to track trajectories of individual pairs changed over time (fig \ref{fig:geo_time}B).
We again see a sharp decrease in transmission across most subdivision pairs from 2020 Q1 to 2020 Q2 which persists through the remainder of 2020.
The $nRR_{seq}$ scores began to increase in a gradated fashion during 2021 as within region nRR scores increased in early 2021 and between region nRR scores increased in late 2021.
International normalized $RR_{seq}$ scores remained low throughout 2021 and had a delayed rise in 2022.
These results suggest that much of the early pandemic transmission tended to be local and slowly expanded across scales.

Given that our analysis had mainly identified within region pairs as enriched for identical sequences, we sought to see if relatively high scoring $nRR_{seq}$ scores could be used to identify between region subdivision pairs with significant transmission throughout the study period.
We define conserved high scoring pairs as between region subdivision pairs whose $nRR_{seq}$ scores were above the 98th percentile (i.e. top second percentile) of for at least five of the twenty quarters during the study time period (shown in fig \ref{fig:geo_time}C).
There were 21 between region pairings that had at least 5 quarters meeting these criteria, and all of these were between US states.
Florida had the most high scoring connections (9) followed by New York and New Jersey (both with 6 connections).
Of specific pairings, Florida - New Jersey and Florida - Pennsylvania were the most common conserved connections, with each having 10 quarters of nRR within the top 98th percentile.
This suggests that Florida acted as a major hub for inter-regional transmission, especially between the Southeast and the Mideast regions.
We then stratify this analysis by quarters to look for between state pairs which had at least 2 years of high scoring $nRR_{seq}$ for each quarter.
We find that the number of conserved connections vary by season with Q1 (January - March) and Q3 (July - September) having the most conserved connections and Q2 (April - June) having the least.
Florida had the most conserved connections across all four quarters, with the majority of these connections tended to be with Mideast states (New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and New York), but also with Texas and California across multiple quarters.
The persistence of Florida's conserved connections year-round highlights the importance it plays in between region transmission patterns across the United States.

In order to better understand the drivers of these geographic relationships, we wished to identify what movement and distance variables could be used to predict $RR_{seq}$.
The variables we investigated include the minimum core-based statistical area (CBSA) distance between states, the number of ground travel trips between states measured in the National Household Travel Survey (NHTS), the timeshare proportion of cellphone users traveling across state lines measured by SafeGraph's mobility data, and the number of air travel passengers traveling between states using the US Department of Transportation's Airline Origin and Destination Survey (DB1B).
This analysis was limited to the continental United States as most of our data sources were US specific, and Hawaii and Alaska were outliers for all distance and movement metrics due to their geographic isolation.
To facilitate better comparisons, we calculate relative risk scores for air travel trips, ground travel trips, and smartphone mobility data and then log-transform all RR scores (including $RR_seq$), while keep geographic distance on a linear scale.
For the air travel, state pairs with no trips were removed from the analysis as they significantly biased the relationship to no correlation when included and bounded to the air travel RR floor.
The results of our analysis are shown in figure \ref{fig:geo_dist}A.
Distance between states is negatively correlated with $RR_{seq}$ and shows a biphasic pattern, with a rapid descent in $RR_{seq}$ from 0 to 500 km and then a slower descent phase beyond 500 km.
This distance coincides with where state pairs transition from being primarily within the same region to where they are in different regions.
Ground travel and mobility RR are both positively correlated with $RR_{seq}$, and both show a transition into a higher slope moving from inter-regional (i.e. higher distance) pairs to intra-regional (i.e. shorter distance).
Air travel has the weakest relationship with $RR_{seq}$ across these variables.
It has a weak positive correlation (r = 0.13), with the best-fit curve initially showing a negative relationship and then a slight positive relationship.
Notably, unlike with ground travel and cellphone mobility, air travel RR was spread evenly across both intra-regional and inter-regional pairs.
These findings suggest that the relationship between movement/travel variables and transmission may behave differently based on the distance between the two states.

To test a possible interaction between movement/travel with distance, we first calculate the correlation of each travel variable with $RR_{seq}$ before and after controlling for CBSA distance (supplemental table: \ref{tab:partial_corr}).
For the interaction analyses, pairs with significant travel/movement outliers (i.e. $\geq 3$ SD) were removed due to these resulting from pairs with sparse data and often being unstable estimates. 
The correlation of ground travel decreases from 0.681 to 0.252 after controlling for distance while the correlation for cellphone mobility decreases from 0.540 to 0.018, indicating much of the relationship of these movement variables overlaps with the relationship explained by distance.
In contrast, air travel has a more modest decrease in correlation from 0.290 to 0.232.
We then calculate stratified correlations based on the state pair's CBSA distance shown in table \ref{tab:band_corr}.
Ground travel and mobility data RR scores are most strongly correlated with $RR_{seq}$ for short distance pairs (0-250 km) with both $r > 0.6$.
For long distance pairs (3000-5000 km) ground travel still remains significantly correlated (r = 0.476), but mobility data became uncorrelated (r = -0.060) at moderate distances (1000-3000 km) and anti-correlated (r = -0.319) at long distances (3000-5000 km).
Therefore, our ability to predict transmission relationships based on cellphone mobility data appears limited to very short distance state pairings, while ground travel had a more consistent signal across a wider geographic range.
Air travel is anti-correlated to $RR_{seq}$ at short distances (r = -0.236) and most strongly related to it at long distances (r = 0.544).
Notably it has the strongest correlation of the three variables for all distance categories greater than 250 km, supporting that air travel data can be helpful in predicting long distance transmission patterns.
Based on these findings we developed generalized additive models (GAMs) with tensor products to look at the interaction of ground travel and air travel with CBSA distance.
The relative performance comparing GAM models with and without distance is shown in \ref{fig:geo_dist}B and observed vs fitted plots for all the GAM models are shown in supplemental figure \ref{fig:obs_exp}.
The distance only model performs the best of all the single predictor models with 50\% of deviance explained followed by ground travel at 40\%.
We find that adding an interaction term decreases RMSE and increases the percentage of deviance explained for ground travel, air travel and cellphone mobility models, and log likelihood ratio testing also confirmed these interaction terms are significant.

Even though the deviance and RMSE terms are similar across the combined models, these predictors behave differently over when fixing the distance between states.
Predictions from the interaction models for ground travel and air travel stratified by distance are shown in figure \ref{fig:geo_dist}C.
For both variables, shorter distance pairs at 100 km have the highest predicted $RR_{seq}$ while longer distance pairs at 2000 km and 4000 km curves predict lower $RR_{seq}$, regardless of the travel RR.
This matches our expectation from plotting $RR_{seq}$ versus distance.
For ground travel, we find a generally monotonic relationship with predicted $RR_{seq}$ across most distances.
The one exception is the 500 km curve, which remains generally flat and centered around $RR_{seq} = 1$.
Air travel is predicted to essentially have no relationship with $RR_{seq}$ when holding the state distance at 100 km, and then positive relationships at medium distances (500 km) and very long distances (4000 km).
Notably, the 500 km curve rises initially and then saturates at $RR_{seq}=1$ once the air travel $RR > 1$.
The 2000 km and 4000 km curves both start closely together with an initial rise, but the 2000 km curve diverges after the air travel $RR > 0.5$ and actually predicts a decrease in $RR_{seq}$ as air travel $RR > 1.0$.
Importantly, these models are only plotted over the range of the travel RRs that existed for each distance band, and these curves should not be extrapolated beyond the plotted extent.
Nonetheless, these plots highlight the complex interplay between distance and travel measures regarding their relationship to viral transmission.

Using a very large number of sequences enables us to describe high resolution transmission patterns between age groups, down to single-year identical sequence relative risks.
We created a full $RR_{seq}$ heatmap across all ages from 0 to 80, shown in figure \ref{fig:age}A, and find several notable age patterns.
Across all ages we find the highest $RR_{seq}$ scores to be among same age pairings (i.e. the heatmap's diagonal) and adjacent age groups, suggesting the bulk of transmission occurs in same age or near same age pairings.
For children (ages < 18 years) we find a very strong transmission cluster with $RR_{seq} > 1$ for nearly all age pairs, except for infants (i.e. 0-year-olds), who have a much smaller transmission cluster limited to ages 0-2.
The younger age findings are shown in-depth in figure \ref{fig:age}B.
Notably there is broad enrichment across all pediatric age groups rather than limited enrichment to an age's specific school group (e.g. primary school or secondary school).
Within this range, $RR_{seq}$ is highest for same-age pairings and then tends to decay across 3-5 years from the diagonal, with a clear boundary at ages $> 17$.
Additionally, there is a weaker cluster of sequence enrichment between children and adults from 30 to 50 years of age, creating a secondary diagonal.
This is evidence of detecting transmission between parental and child age groups.
For 18-year-olds there is a clear transition in transmission patterns away from children and towards young adults.
We find a discrete block of enrichment for 18 to 22-year-olds, with $RR_{seq}$ greater than 1.1 for all pairs in this discrete block.
This block corresponds to the typical age of university students across North America and when adults generally enter the workforce if they do not pursue tertiary education.
From age 23 onwards, the enrichment pattern becomes similar to the general adult population with a narrow band of 1-3 years around the diagonal where $RR_{seq}$ remains greater than 1.1 and then a wider band of $RR_{seq} \approx 1$,
Finally, for age 60 years and onwards we see another transmission cluster emerge, with $RR_{seq}$ increasing with age.
The identified transmission clusters seem to correspond to age contact behaviors, and we compared our $RR_{seq}$ scores to existing synthetic contact data (supplementary fig\ref{fig:age_contact}).\autocite{mistryInferringHighresolutionHuman2021}
Both for Canada ($r=0.60$) and the USA ($r=0.72$), $RR_{seq}$ was log-log correlated to the contact data, supporting this hypothesis.

Additionally, we wanted to test how these age relationships behaved at geographic levels.
We stratify the age $RR_{seq}$ heatmaps in figure \ref{fig:age}C by same division pairs, different division but same region pairs and different region pairs.
For the same state age heatmap, the structure largely matches our main age heatmap with a prominent diagonal of $RR_{seq} > 1.5$, and distinct clusters for children, university aged young adults and elderly adults.
As we look at more distant geographic connections, we find that the age structure becomes less prominent with the central diagonal reverting to $RR_{seq} = 1$ for adults and greater mixing of the pediatric and elderly clusters with the general population.
In order to systematically summarize how much a given age group's $RR_{seq}$ profile deviates from random mixing, we created a deviance score.
This is calculated by finding the root-mean-square of the age group's log-transformed RR scores:

\[D_A = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{B} \left( \log_{10} RR_{seq}^{A,B} \right)^2}\]

Where $A$ and $B$ represent age groups in the population or any other deme definition.
By squaring the log-transformed $RR_{seq}$, enrichment ($RR_{seq} > 1$) and depletion ($RR_{seq} < 1$) contribute equally to the deviance.
We expect that if for age $A$'s identical sequences are fully randomly mixed (i.e. $RR_{seq}^{A,B} = 1$ across all paired ages $B$) that the deviance, $D_A$, would equal 0.
As age structured transmission increases the deviance will increase with there being no fixed upper bound for $D_A$.
The deviance for each age category across different geographies is shown in figure $\ref{fig:age}D$.
The same division deviance curve shows age structured transmission is highest for the young and the elderly, with the minimum in middle adulthood.
For the other geographies we see deviance remains relatively increased among the young and elderly age groups, but the magnitude of deviance scores are decreased compared to the same division curve across all ages.
These deviance curves are evidence that age structure plays a stronger role in transmission patterns at local geographies, with the age contact matrix loosening for transmission across individuals from different geographies.

\section{Discussion}

As we discussed earlier, traditional phylogeographic methods are generally not feasible for conducting an analysis including hundreds of thousands or millions of sequences.
Previously, we have shown that the relative risk of identical sequences was able to describe relationships across a large dataset of 114,298 sequences from Washington state.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}
A major motivation for this paper was to test this method's computational scalability at a multinational scale.
There were two main computational challenges we were faced with when scaling up to 5.7 million sequences: identifying all identical pairs within the dataset of comparisons and parsing large data structures to calculate the relative risks.
Our computational approach to overcome these challenges involves preprocessing the sequence data for storage in a portable relational database.
Since the relative risk calculation is basic arithmetic after the distance matrix is calculated, relational databases are ideal as they can be used to annotate and query identical sequence pairs for the demes of interest.
This allows rapid calculation of $RR_{seq}$ across different demes and strata with low memory cost.
While the preprocessing step does require significant computing power (i.e. workstation with large amount of memory and multiprocessing or a computational cluster), the actual relative risk calculations and all downstream analyses can be done on local machines.
Details of this technique and the hardware that we used to run this analysis can be found in the supplementary information.

One major benefit of being able to conduct these analyses so efficiently is that we are able to do high resolution geographic analyses even across a large area.
Traditional phylogeographic approaches would not be able to process a sufficient number of sequences to describe the relationships between all 62 geographic units in our analyses.
Instead, approaches have typically needed to either focus on a smaller area study area for a high resolution analysis or subsample data from a larger area and then aggregate locations.
One major drawback of this approach is that aggregation implicitly assumes a degree of homogeneity within that population and can miss relationships.
For example, it may seem reasonable to assume that the states within pre-defined US regions would have similar transmission patterns based on geographic/economic proximity.
However, in our analysis, we found heterogeneity in the transmission Southeast and Far West states, with states being more similar to states from other regions.
When we repeated the RR analysis at the regional level instead of state/province (supplemental fig \ref{fig:reg_hmap}) the Far West had no $RR_{seq}$ scores > 1 with any other regions, despite individual states having $RR_{seq} > 1$ with geographies outside the same region (e.g. Washington and Idaho, California and Mexico). 
This highlights how geographic aggregation can miss the actual scale and structure where transmission occurs.

An important example of the significance of scale can be seen with the ``Acela Corridor'' in the United States, where five major metropolitan areas span the northeastern coast: Boston, MA; New York, NY; Philadelphia, PA; Baltimore, MD and Washington DC.
Since US metropolitan areas often cross state boundaries, this can lead them to spill across regions as well.
Based on this we expected Connecticut (within the New England region) to have high levels of transmission with New York (Mideast) due to having a large commuting population to New York City.
Similarly, we expected Maryland (within the Mideast) to have high levels of transmission with Virginia (Southeast) as they both are part of the Washington DC metropolitan area.
Both of these relationships are confirmed in our state level heatmap.
If we zoom our hierarchical clustering tree to focus on the New England, Mideast, and Southeast region (supplemental fig \ref{fig:hclust_subset}), we find that the states from Connecticut through South Carolina sit on closely linked branches of the hierarchical tree.
Despite Connecticut's geographical proximity to the New England states, its RR pattern more closely aligns with the New York and Philadelphia metro areas.
Similarly, Virginia, North Carolina and South Carolina are linked more closely to the Washington DC metro states rather than the other Southeastern states.
Identification of these metropolitan transmission clusters match with prior epidemiology and modeling work that discuss the importance of city and metropolitan areas as spatial units for analysis.\autocite{starkLocalVariationsSpatial2012,dalzielUrbanizationHumidityShape2018}
Ideally we would have explored metropolitan or county level transmission patterns, but sub-state and provincial location data was sparse and inconsistently annotated.
Future genomic surveillance data would benefit from consistently including additional geographic resolution such as US counties or Canadian census divisions.

We additionally found that the relationship between movement/travel variables and SARS-CoV-2 was dependent on geographic scale and distance.
Cell phone mobility data, air travel flows, and commuting flows have been used to model the spread of SARS-CoV-2 cases and the timing of importation events.\autocite{kuoAirlineTransportationArrival2021,jiaPopulationFlowDrives2020,zhanModelingPrediction20192020,badrAssociationMobilityPatterns2020a,changMobilityNetworkModels2021}
These models do well at empirically describing the epidemic curves for SARS-CoV-2, but it has been difficult to validate that the measured movement flows correspond to genetic linkage of the virus.
Using the $RR_{seq}$ between US states, we successfully show that these travel variables predict transmission and are able to discriminate different distance regimes where each was pertinent.
We found that cellphone mobility data performed well at short distances, but rapidly lost predictive power for longer distance state pairs.
In contrast, the ground trip data from the NHTS was robust across both short and long distance pairs.
States may be too large of a geographical unit for the cellphone mobility data to perform well, and ideally we would wish to repeat this analysis at the county or even smaller level to see if it outperforms the NHTS trip dataset.
Air travel was associated with transmission for long distance pairs only, outperforming NHTS ground travel flows in our stratified long range analysis.
This is notable as there has been debate regarding the importance of air travel networks versus commuting networks in modeling domestic respiratory virus spread within the United States.\autocite{brownsteinEmpiricalEvidenceEffect2006, viboudSynchronyWavesSpatial2006,charuHumanMobilitySpatial2017}
Our findings agree with a recent phylogeographic analysis of influenza seasons, which found that commuting was the strongest predictor of state-to-state transmission while air travel still contributed additional viral migration.\autocite{dejongCompetitionTransmissionLineages2025}
Future epidemiological models likely would benefit from combining multiple of these movement/travel variables and weighting them by distance to better estimate transmission between cities versus between states/provinces.

The large number of sequences we used in this analysis allowed us to describe age transmission down to single-year age groups, which allows closer investigation of how age related social structures affect transmission.
The most significant examples of this in our study were of children and young adults.
The $RR_{seq}$ heatmap demonstrated a clear transition point in the transmission matrix with 18-year-olds clustering more tightly to university-age young adults instead of with secondary school teenagers.
Similarly, there is a more gradual transition in transmission patterns from age 22 to 23 which corresponds to when many young adults enter the general workforce.
The distinct cluster from 18 to 22 years likely is due in part to the unique transmission environment of university life with its relative social isolation from other age groups, although we cannot directly determine the housing status and education/work background from the metadata provided.
Additionally, we were able to capture signal of a parent-child "secondary diagonal" which has been predicted by prior contact survey and synthetic population studies.\autocite{mossongSocialContactsMixing2008,premProjectingContactMatrices2021,mistryInferringHighresolutionHuman2021} 
This highlights the important contribution of household transmission.
Further work is necessary to determine if the directionality of transmission (adult to child versus child to adult) can be determined based on the sequence metadata, but this will be challenging without paired household data.  
We also saw some evidence for a potential grandparent-infant relationship with 0-year-olds having multiple $RR_{seq}$ > 1 with ages 70-80 years old.
However, age 0 was undersampled (n = 5,129 sequences) compared to neighboring age groups (ages 1-3 ranged from n= 12,388 to 15,026) and other toddler ages do not show a similar intergenerational pattern with elderly adults.
Furthermore, its unclear what role misclassification played for age 0 as it could act as a placeholder value for unknown age sequence.
Therefore, some caution is necessary in interpreting its relationship to other ages.

Overall, the age identical sequence matrix was correlated with the synthetic age contact matrices, validating their utility in predicting transmission patterns.\autocite{mistryInferringHighresolutionHuman2021}
Notably the slope of the $RR_{seq}$ versus contact matrix RR was less than zero for both the US and Canada.
This could suggest that transmission occurs more randomly than expected based on contact data.
However, this may also be an artifact of the sampling dynamics of the pandemic, with disproportionate sampling during late 2021 and early 2022 as the Omicron sweep occurred.
Our age analysis also highlighted that age structure was more important for within division transmission and that age transmission matrix regressed towards $RR = 1$ as the geographic scale expanded.
This suggests that when modeling outbreaks, age structure can be simplified/omitted for transmission across large scales but high resolution matrices are important for the local dynamics.

Normalized $RR_{seq} $was used to identify changes in transmission patterns over time such as seeing if transmission changed across geographic scales over the course of the pandemic.
We find a sequential increase in transmission across from intra-regional to international state/province pairs (fig \ref{fig:geo_time}A-B) from 2020 to 2022, suggesting that transmission patterns followed a graduated restoring of movement and travel patterns.
This likely was a response to multiple factors including loosening of travel restrictions, perceived immunity after vaccination/natural infection, and decreased perception of COVID-19's severity.
Additionally, we tested this normalized $RR_{seq}$ to look at changes in the transmission of the elderly after the vaccine rollout in January 2021 and transmission patterns of school age children throughout the pandemic (see supplemental figure \ref{fig:age_time}).
Both of these analyses looking at changes in transmission across ages were able to detect changes, but their interpretation is somewhat more challenging as host immune profiles including vaccination, social patterns of interaction and schooling/work behaviors were dynamically changing through much of the time periods of interest.  
Ultimately, we are limited by the use of observational data in terms of identifying causality. 
Even with these limitations, the normalized $RR_{seq}$ allows for description of transmission trends and has the benefit of being computationally easy to calculate.
With sufficient pathogen surveillance, it could be used to test the response to policy changes or other interventions in an outbreak.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data Sources}
5,765,925 human SARS-CoV-2 genomes were obtained from the EpiCoV database for Canada, Mexico, and the United States of America from January 2020 through December 2024.\autocite{khareGISAIDsRolePandemic2021}
We limited genomes to be from human hosts, have a valid collection date, and a minimum of 90\% sequencing coverage.
Additionally, for sequences from the United States and Canada, the division field needed to be filled with a valid US state or Canadian province name.
Genomes coming from US territories other than the District of Columbia were excluded due to small sample size.
Age metadata was checked to be numeric and any age value greater than 125 years treated as invalid, with ages greater than 99 were aggregated into a single age category.

We also collected several distance, movement, and contact variables to look for associations with identical sequence RR ($RR_{seq}$).
State geographic distance was defined to be the minimum geographic distance between core-based statistical areas (CBSAs) as defined by the US Census Bureau.\autocite{bureauMetropolitanMicropolitanStatistical}
Ground travel RR scores between states were calculated from the 2017 National Household Travel Survey's day trip file.\autocite{NationalHouseholdTravel}
We calculated air travel RR using the 2019 Airline Origin and Destination Survey (DB1B).\autocite{DB1B_Dataset}
Cellphone timeshare data collected by SafeGraph was used to create a mobility RR score between states.\autocite{pullanoCharacterizingUSSpatial2025}
Finally we used synthetic contact matrices based on survey data to compare with age seqRR in the US and Canada.\autocite{mistryInferringHighresolutionHuman2021}

\subsection{SARS-CoV-2 Genome Processing}
Identical sequences were identified using a similar pipeline to our previous work.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}
First a distance matrix of sequences was created.
In order to minimize unnecessary comparisons, we only calculated distances for sequences within the same Nextstrain clade.\autocite{aksamentovNextcladeCladeAssignment2021}
Within each clade, \texttt{pairsnp} was used to find all pairs of sequences with a Hamming distance equal to 0.\autocite{tonkin-hillGtonkinhillPairsnp2025}
Sequences that were found to have over 1,000 identical pairs were removed from the analysis due to being extreme outliers.
These sequences typically have missing bases at sites with key mutations and therefore an inflated number of matching identical sequences.
Additionally, sequence pairs with sequences that were collected within 3 days of each other and had the same host metadata (age, gender, division/location) were flagged as likely being sequences from the same host and excluded from the relative risk calculation.
The final sequence metadata and list of identical pairs are then stored in a DuckDB relational database, which allowed for fast database operations without loading the full list into system memory.
This database was accessed within the R programming environment using the \texttt{dbplyr} library.\autocite{rcoreteamLanguageEnvironmentStatistical2025,wickhamDbplyrDplyrBack2025}

\subsection{Identical Sequence Relative Risk Calculation}
We use our previously described relative risk calculation to measure the genetic connectivity of SARS-CoV-2 between different geographic units and age groups.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}
We define $n_{A,B}$ to be the number of pairs of genomes with a Hamming distance of 0 where one sequence belongs to group $A$ and the other sequence belongs to group $B$.
We then define $n_{A,\bullet}= \sum_{\forall B}n_{A,B}$ (i.e. all pairs of identical sequences where at least one sequence is in group $A$) and $n_{\bullet,\bullet} = \sum_{\forall A}n_{A,\bullet}$ (i.e. the total number of pairs of identical sequences).
We calculate the $RR_{seq}^{A,B}$ as follows:

\[RR_{seq}^{A,B} = \frac{n_{A,B}/n_{A,\bullet}}{n_{B,\bullet}/n_{\bullet,\bullet}}\]

$RR_{seq}$ scores were calculated for geography and age.
Additionally, we were able to use this approach to subset the population and look at the interaction between multiple variables at the same time (e.g. the $RR_{seq}$ of individuals with the same age within the same state/province compared to the $RR_{seq}$ of individuals with the same age not in the same state/province).
We calculated uncertainty through subsampling 80\% of the total sequences without replacement to create a 95\% confidence interval.\autocite{tran-kiemFinescalePatternsSARSCoV22025a}

We extended this approach to describe changes in $RR_{seq}$ over time.
Let $RR_{seq}^{a,b}(t)$ be the $RR_{seq}^{a,b}$ for pairs whose collection date fall between $t$ and $t + \Delta t$, with $\Delta t$ being some pre-decided interval width.
Changes in $RR_{seq}$ can be due to true changes in the transmission pattern between demes $A$ and $B$, but it can also be due to changes in the baseline force of infection of either the pathogen (i.e. variants having differences in pathogenicity) or the host populations (i.e. behavioral/movement changes, immunizations).
We therefore normalize $RR_{seq}$ against a baseline population to account for these changes in the force of infection.

For time analyses of the age $RR_{seq}$, our objective was to detect changes in transmission across age groups due to changes in interactions between the groups (e.g. school closures, movement restrictions) or if one age group acquired immunity (e.g. vaccination).
Prior studies have shown that during the initial phases of the COVID-19 pandemic, young adults (i.e. 18-40 year olds) tended to have lower levels of social isolation and risk mitigation behaviors compared to other age groups.\autocite{kimHowDoesAge2020,helenaj.hutchinsCOVID19MitigationBehaviors2020,engeliSocialContactPatterns}
Therefore we used adults as a reference group (designated here as $Y$) for normalization.
We then normalized other $RR_{seq}$ scores over time using the following equation: $nRR_{seq}^{A,B}(t) = RR_{seq}^{A,B}(t)/RR_{seq}^{Y,Y}(t)$.
As a result, $nRR_{seq}^{Y,Y}(t) = 1$ for all $t$.

For time analyses of geographic $RR_{seq}$, changes in the circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants, public health interventions, and sequence sampling effort over time could affect the number of genomes collected from a specific area.
Additionally, there is a challenge that there is no single geographic area that we can use as a reference population in this case.
Since our goal was to determine if we could find how much mixing changed between populations over time we used the average of the within division $RR_{seq}$ for the two divisions as the normalizing factor.
This can be written as follows:
\begin{align*}
nRR_{seq}^{A,B}(t) &= \frac{RR_{seq}^{A,B}(t)}{\frac{1}{2}(RR_{seq}^{A,A}(t)+RR_{seq}^{B,B}(t))} \\
             &= \frac{2 RR_{seq}^{A,B}(t)}{RR_{seq}^{A,A}(t)+RR_{seq}^{B,B}(t)}
\end{align*}
It is important to note that with this normalization, there is not a clear scale that indicates whether a division pairing has enrichment of identical sequences.
Since $RR_{seq}^{A,A}$ and $RR_{seq}^{B,B}$ tend to typically be greater than $RR_{seq}^{A,B}$ we expect $nRR_{seq}^{A,B} < 1$ and really focus on its utility to look at changes over time and in comparison to other across division pairings.
We used agent-based modeling where we simulate nucleotide mutation simulations to explore and validate this approach.
This included creating scenarios where movement changed between simulated demes and introducing changes to the force of infection.
Further discussion of these validation simulations can be found in the supplementary information.

\subsection{Geographic Clustering and Regional Analysis}

States/provinces were sorted using hierarchical clustering to assess if states/provinces tended to be most related to other states within the same region.
For clustering and dimensionality reduction, we convert $RR_{seq}^{A,B}$ to a dissimilarity score between $A$ and $B$: $\exp{(-RR_{seq}^{A,B})}$ which is then used as a distance matrix for clustering analyses.
Hierarchical clustering of states and provinces is calculated using the agglomerative nesting (AGNES) algorithm from the \texttt{cluster} package.\autocite{maechlerClusterClusterAnalysis2026}
Principal coordinate analysis (i.e. classical multidimensional scaling) is conducted with the \texttt{cmdscale} function from the base \texttt{stats} package.\autocite{rcoreteamLanguageEnvironmentStatistical2025}
The absolute eigenvalue proportion was used for estimating the percent variance of each principal coordinate.
We also sought to identify between regional state/province pairs that were persistently high scoring over time.
Since the geographic $nRR_{seq}$ does not have a clear threshold for enrichment, across region division pairs were ranked into percentiles.
Pairs with five or more quarters of being ranked in the 98th percentile or higher were defined to be high-ranking across region pairs.
Additionally, if pairs had two or more years of being ranked in the 98th percentile for a specific quarter they were flagged as a significant seasonal relationship.

\subsection{Distance/Mobility Regression Analysis}

For the continental United States, identical sequence relative risk scores were compared to multiple measures of distance and movement.
Pearson's correlation coefficients between identical sequence RR and these distance/movement variables were calculated, where all RR variables (both $RR_{seq}$ and travel/movement) were first transformed to a base-10 logarithmic scale.
Same-state $RR_{seq}$ scores were excluded as were any pairs of states with a reported travel or movement score of zero.
To test for interactions between state distance and travel/movement variables we calculated partial correlation coefficients after controlling for distance and distance stratified correlation coefficients.
Finally, we constructed generalized additive models to predict the sequence $\log(RR_{seq})$ with tensor product smooths of the interaction between CBSA distance with movement variables, using the \texttt{mgcv} package in R.\autocite{woodGeneralizedAdditiveModels2017}

\subsection{Validating Relative Risk Simulations}
To test the nRR approach, we developed a simulation of an outbreak in a three population system: $O$, $A$, and $B$.
Agent based simulation was done using \texttt{nosoi} to create a phylogenetic transmission tree.\autocite{lequimeNosoiStochasticAgentbased2020}
Agents had a baseline movement probability of 0.02, with agents in $O$ having a 50\% chance of traveling to either $A$ or $B$ if they moved.
Meanwhile, agents in $A$ and $B$ had an 80\% chance of going to $O$ and 20\% chance of going to either $B$ or $A$, respectively.
Each population had a carrying capacity 500 infected agents.
Infected agents had a mean of 1.5 contacts per time step drawn according to a Poisson distribution.
The transmission probability for each contact was based on a Beta distribution with $\alpha = 4, \beta = 8$ with an incubation period drawn from a Gamma distribution.
There was a 1 in 7 chance at each time step for an infected agent to exit the simulation (i.e. recover).

We completed 50 simulations where the movement probability for $A$ was set to 0 at $t=150$ for 200 time steps to simulate a lockdown condition.
For each simulation, an outbreak would originate from an infected agent in $O$ and the simulation would continue until either 30,000 infections occurred or more than 1,000 time steps passed.
From \texttt{nosoi} we were able to obtain a Newick transmission tree which was then passed to \texttt{phastSim} to simulate nucleotide evolution.
We used a mutation rate of $2 \times 10^{-6}$ substitutions per site per day to match a similar rate as SARS-CoV-2.\autocite{tayEmergenceSARSCoV2Variants2022}
These simulated sequences were then fed into a similar pipeline as described earlier to calculate the RR and nRR between the three geographic areas over time.
The full results of this analysis can be seen in Figure.

\subsection{Computational Approach}
First we needed to calculate a full list of pairs of identical sequences using \texttt{pairSNP}.
Calculating the distance matrix between all sequences would have resulted in ~16.6 trillion comparisons, but we are able to dramatically reduce this by: 1) limiting \texttt{pairSNP} to only calculate zero distance pairs and 2) only calculating the distance matrix for sequences within the same pango lineage.
Using a 32 core Xeon server with 160 GB RAM allocated, we are able to run all the pre-processing steps and matrix calculation in roughly 36-48 hours.
The next challenge was the calculation of the relative risk scores.
While the calculation of a relative risk is a simple operation, counting the number of pairs within demes and annotating the pairs with pertinent metadata is challenging due to the size of the datasets (4.0 GB for the list of sequence pairs and 7.7 GB for the metadata dataframe).
Loading both of these files into an R session's memory can push a typical desktop or laptop machine to the limits of its memory and reduce performance.
Therefore, we combine these files into a portable SQL database and use DuckDB via the \texttt{dbplyr} package to complete all the queries needed for the RR calculation.
The resulting database is smaller than either file (1.5 GB) and RR calculation performance is excellent.
Calculating the full RR matrix takes 7-8 seconds for the single year age bins and 11-12 seconds for the state/province bins on a M1 Macbook Pro.
Therefore, after the initial pre-processing steps on a large workstation or scientific cluster, specific analyses can be run on personal machines with relative ease.
The portable database also allows for easy sharing of data between multiple team members and DuckDB will automatically use parallel processing to accelerate analyses.
The main drawback of the SQL database arises when creating bootstrap samples for RR confidence intervals, as there is some performance overheard for DuckDB + \texttt{dbplyr} queries compared to running the command directly in R.
For analyses where only a subset of the total sequences are necessary for RR calculation (such as the time-based analyses), we can use a hybrid approach of pulling the subset from DuckDB into R's memory and then doing the sampling and analysis within R.
This combined approach lets us run the entire vaccine period analysis in Figure \ref{fig:age_time}A in approximately 4 minutes, even with 200 bootstrap samples per time point.

\subsection{Air Travel Definition: DB1B vs T100}
We also investigated using the US Department of Transportation's T-100 market dataset to measure air travel flows.
The T-100 set measures each travel leg volumes instead of looking at flows between boarding origin and destinations.
It is not possible to tell which passengers arrive at their final destination versus a layover between flights, making interpretation of flows difficult.
While the raw correlation between T-100 and identical sequence RRs was higher compared to the DB1B dataset (r = 0.26), we ultimately felt this approach misclassified flows as many trips between non-hub cities will require a layover at a regional hub first.
When looking for correlation across our travel/distance variables (fig \ref{fig:pred_corr}) we find that the T-100 RR scores tend to be more correlated with NHTS ground travel data and CBSA distance than with the DB1B ticket data.
Furthermore, the partial correlation of the T-100 market data with identical sequence RR after controlling for distance is weaker than the DB1B partial correlation (0.113 vs 0.232).
These findings suggest that using T-100 segment leg flows captures much of the same identical sequence RR variance as distance and adds less new information compared to the DB1B data.

\subsection{Additional Distance/Movement Interaction Analyses}

To quantify the unique contribution of each movement metric beyond geographic distance, we computed Pearson's correlations between identical-sequence $\log_{10}(\text{RR})$ and each $\log_{10}$-transformed travel/mobility RR, both marginally and after partialling out minimum CBSA distance (Table~\ref{tab:partial_corr}).
Partial correlations were obtained by correlating the residuals of each variable after regression on a thin-plate spline smooth of distance $s(\text{CBSA dist.})$, which accounts for the strongly nonlinear effect of distance.
Once distance is accounted for, SafeGraph mobility retains essentially no independent association ($r=0.02$), while ground travel (NHTS) and origin--destination air travel (DB1B) retain the largest unique signal.
Band-stratified correlations (Table~\ref{tab:band_corr}) show that the movement-transmission association is concentrated at short range for mobility and at long range for air travel.

The significance of the distance$\times$movement interaction was assessed by likelihood-ratio-style tests comparing an additive tensor-spline model, $\text{ti(movement)} + \text{ti(CBSA dist.)}$, against a model adding the interaction term $\text{ti(movement, CBSA dist.)}$, fit by maximum likelihood with the \texttt{mgcv} package (Table~\ref{tab:interaction_lrt}). The interaction was highly significant for every movement metric in both the negative-binomial count and Gaussian $\log_{10}(\text{RR})$ formulations.

\subsection{Changes in Age Based Identical Sequence RR Over Time}

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{age_time.pdf}
    \caption{Temporal changes in SARS-CoV-2 transmission patterns between ages, using identical sequence RR normalized against the working-age adults (25-64y) within-group RR ($nRR$) (see Methods).
    A: Normalized $RR_{seq}$ between elderly groups and other age groups during the vaccine rollout period (November 2020--May 2021).
    Each trace shows the normalized $RR_{seq}$ between an elderly index group (65--80y, 80+y) and all partner age groups across rolling two-month windows, with shaded 95\% subsampled confidence intervals.
    B: Within-state Normalized $RR_{seq}$ between school-age children and other age groups 3-month seasons pooled across all states.
    Each trace shows the normalized $RR_{seq}$ between a school-age index group (5--11y, 12--17y) and all partner age groups, with shaded 95\% subsampled confidence intervals.
    The dashed line at $nRR = 1$ indicates when the RR is equal to the working-age baseline. For both plots, the confidence intervals used 200 subsamples per time point with 80\% coverage.
    }
    \label{fig:age_time}
\end{figure}

We investigated whether temporal changes in age-based $RR_{seq}$ could be used to detect contact structure changes in response to public health interventions.
First we sought to see if the transmission patterns for elderly adults changed during the initial COVID-19 vaccine rollout period (Winter 2020 through Spring 2021), shown in figure \ref{fig:age_time}A.
For all three countries, vaccine rollout was initially prioritized to individuals with medical comorbidities, older age, and residence in a long-term care (LTC) facility.
Therefore, we focused on the normalized $RR_{seq}$ for the 65-80 and 80+ age groups across rolling two-month windows from November 2020 to May 2021, using the within-group $RR_{seq}$ of 25-64 year-olds as the reference category.
We find that for the 65-80 group (left panel) transmission was highest with the 65-80 and 80+ groups, as would be expected from our age heatmap.
Notably there are minimal differences between these two curves across all months, and both show a transient increase from 1.3-1.4 in November 2020 to a peak of 1.7-1.8 in late February.
For the 80+ group (right panel), within-group transmission is significantly higher than transmission between the 65-80 and 80+ groups, with the within-group normalized $RR_{seq}$ being 2-4 fold higher.
The 80+ within-group normalized $RR_{seq}$ dropped from 6.4 in November 2020 to 3.5 in January 2021 and then fluctuated between roughly 3.5 and 5 for the remainder of the period.
All three countries introduced the COVID-19 vaccine in December 2020, consistent with the timing of the decrease in 80+ transmission.
However, we do not see corresponding transmission structure changes in the 65-80 group, and the decrease in the 80+ group is transient.
The significance of this is drop is difficult to ascertain due to the wider confidence intervals for the 80+/80+ $RR_{seq}$ during this time period.
The lack of a drop in the 65-80 curves may be because much of this group was vaccinated on a similar schedule to the broader adult population, whereas the 80+ group was vaccinated earlier.
Furthermore, the 80+ age category has the highest proportion of LTC residents of any age group, which would have increased its vaccination priority and helped with rapid vaccine deployment in this population.
These normalized $RR_{seq}$ curves may also be affected by non-vaccination changes in transmission, such as increased isolation of the older population and differential rates of natural infection and immunity across populations.
Notably, the normalized $RR_{seq}$ only measures relative changes in transmission and cannot be used to describe absolute changes in incidence.
Ultimately, we did find an age-specific change in transmission within the 80+ group that may have been tied to the vaccine rollout, but the signal is not definitive as it was isolated to a single age group pairing.

We also were interested in how the age transmission patterns of school age children (ages 5-11 and 12-17) changed over the pandemic.
Schooling transitioned to virtual and hybrid models for many states and provinces across all three countries starting from spring 2020 and throughout the 2020-2021 school year.\autocite{COVID19ProvincialPolicies, DataResourcesCOVID19, boruchowiczTimeUseYouth2022}
Therefore, we were curious to see if within group transmission for school age children would decrease during these time periods.
Normalized $RR_{seq}$ were calculated in fixed three-month windows corresponding to seasons: Winter (December to February), Spring (March to May), Summer (June to August), and Autumn (September to November), with the results shown in Figure \ref{fig:age_time}B.
We limit our analysis specifically to same state/province pairs to focus on the role of local transmission (e.g. within school and household).
For both school age groups we find transmission to be generally higher with other pediatric age groups (0-4, 5-11, 12-17) than with the adult age groups throughout the time course of the pandemic, and the within-group nRR curves are the highest.
The earliest pandemic seasons (spring and summer 2020) were calculated based on very few within-state pediatric pairs (fewer than 100 per season) and produce extremely noisy nRR estimates that are difficult to interpret.
From fall 2020 onward, the pediatric nRR curves were suppressed throughout the 2020-2021 school year, with the within-group normalized $RR_{seq}$ for both school-age groups around 1.5 by spring 2021 and the between-pediatric-group nRR (e.g. 12-17 with 0-4) regressing to the working-age baseline (normalized $RR_{seq}$ $\approx$ 1).
The within-group curves then rose as in-person schooling became the dominant modality in the 2021-2022 school year and continued to rise through the 2022-2023 school year.
After summer 2023, the normalized seqRR became increasingly noisy again as pair counts decreased (down to the tens to low hundreds of pairs), demonstrated by the increasing width of the subsampled confidence intervals around this time.
We are able to find some evidence of decreased school age transmission for the 2020-2021 compared to the 2021-2022 and 2022-2023 school years, suggesting that school closures may have reduced the force of transmission between children.
However, in-person schooling policies were highly variable across the geographies which limits our ability to draw any causal relationship between these policies and the change in transmission patterns.
Additionally, if immunity was higher in the older populations due to vaccination and earlier natural infection, this would have increased the relative susceptibility of younger age groups and caused an apparent increased force of infection.
